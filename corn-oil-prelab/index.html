Part A

The molecules

Fatty acids, and the fats and oils they are stored in.

1Fatty acids

A fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain with a carboxylic acid at one end. Almost everything about a fat, from whether it is solid to how it runs on a TLC plate, comes from that chain.

The carboxyl carbon is carbon 1. Natural fatty acids have an even number of carbons (most often 16 or 18), because cells build them two carbons at a time. A chain with only single bonds is saturated (it holds as many hydrogens as it can); a chain with one or more C=C double bonds is unsaturated.

Chemists write a fatty acid in shorthand: the number of carbons, a colon, the number of double bonds, then where each double bond starts, counted from carbon 1. Oleic acid is 18:1 cis-9: 18 carbons, one double bond, between carbons 9 and 10, cis. (Nutrition labels count from the other end, the methyl or ω end: oleic acid is then an ω-9 fatty acid.)

In natural fatty acids the double bonds are almost always cis: the two parts of the chain leave the double bond on the same side, which puts a permanent bend of about 30° in the chain. When there are several, they are three carbons apart, with one CH2 between them (“methylene-interrupted”).

Pick a fatty acid

Why the bends matter: melting points

Straight saturated chains lie side by side like pencils in a box, touching along their whole length; the many weak attractions between them (London dispersion forces) hold them in a solid. A cis bend stops the chains from packing closely, so there is less contact, and less heat is needed to melt them. Two trends follow, and you can see both in the chart: the longer the chain, the higher it melts; the more cis double bonds, the lower it melts.

  • Shorthand C:D cis-n: carbons, double bonds, where each starts counting from the carboxyl carbon.
  • Natural double bonds are cis and bend the chain; bent chains pack poorly and melt low.
  • Longer chains melt higher; more double bonds melt lower.

Check yourself

  1. Write the shorthand of a fatty acid with 16 carbons and one cis double bond between carbons 9 and 10.
    Answer
    16:1 cis-9 (this is palmitoleic acid).
  2. Without looking at the chart, put these in order of melting point, lowest first: 18:0, 18:2, 14:0.
    Answer
    18:2 (two bends) < 14:0 (straight but short) < 18:0 (straight and long).
  3. Margarine was once made by adding hydrogen to some of an oil’s double bonds. Why does that turn a liquid oil into a soft solid?
    Answer
    Fewer double bonds means fewer bends: the chains pack more closely and the fat melts higher. (Partial hydrogenation also turned some cis bonds into straight trans ones, the “trans fats”.)

2Fats and oils are triglycerides

Fatty acids are rarely free in a fat. Three of them are joined as esters to one molecule of glycerol: a triglyceride (also called a triacylglycerol).

Glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) has three OH groups. Each one can form an ester with the carboxyl group of a fatty acid, losing a water each time. A natural fat is a mixture of many different triglycerides, each carrying its own combination of three fatty acids. A fat that is liquid at room temperature is called an oil; the difference is mostly how many of its chains are bent.

Build a triglyceride

Choose the fatty acid on each carbon of glycerol. The page works out the formula mass and how much potassium hydroxide it takes to break all three esters.

  • A triglyceride is glycerol with three fatty acids attached by ester bonds.
  • To get the fatty acids out, those three esters must be broken: that is the first job of the experiment.
  • Every triglyceride needs three hydroxides to break its three esters.

Check yourself

  1. Tripalmitin is glycerol carrying three palmitic acids (M = 256.42 each). Work out its molar mass. (Glycerol 92.09, water 18.02.)
    Answer
    92.09 + 3(256.42) − 3(18.02) = 807.3 g/mol. Build it in the tool to check.
  2. How many millimoles of KOH does it take to break every ester in 0.500 g of tripalmitin?
    Answer
    0.500 g ÷ 807.3 g/mol = 0.619 mmol of fat; three KOH each: 1.86 mmol KOH. Try the same sum for your own 0.10 mL of oil and the 2.0 mL of 0.5 M KOH you will add: is there enough to break every ester? (An oil’s density is about 0.92 g/mL; take its molar mass as about 875 g/mol.)

Part B

The chemistry

Four reactions and separations, in the order you will meet them.

3Saponification: breaking the esters

Warming a fat with potassium hydroxide in ethanol cuts each ester bond. The products are glycerol and the potassium salts of the fatty acids: soap. (Sapo is Latin for soap.)

This is base-promoted ester hydrolysis, a nucleophilic acyl substitution. Ethanol is used as the solvent because it dissolves both the oily fat and the ionic KOH, so they meet in one solution; warming at 55 °C makes it fast enough to finish in 20 minutes. Step through the mechanism for one ester (R is the fatty acid’s chain, R′O is the glycerol side).

The mechanism, one step at a time

The key is the last step. A carboxylic acid (pKa about 5) next to an alkoxide (whose alcohol has a pKa about 16) is an acid next to a much stronger base: the proton moves at once, and the carboxylate left behind has no way back. That is why base hydrolysis goes to completion, while the acid-catalyzed reactions of topic 6 stop at an equilibrium. It is also why it needs a full equivalent of base for each ester: the hydroxide is used up, not given back.

triglyceride + 3 KOH → glycerol + 3 RCOO−K+the handout writes it for three stearic acid chains, C₁₇H₃₅COO−K+

  • Hydroxide adds to the carbonyl, the alkoxide leaves, and a fast proton transfer leaves a carboxylate: no way back.
  • One KOH is used up for every ester broken.
  • After saponification the fatty acids are ions (RCOO−K+), not neutral acids.

Check yourself

  1. Why is ethanol, and not water, the solvent for the KOH?
    Answer
    The fat does not dissolve in water; it would float as a separate layer and react only at the surface. Ethanol dissolves both the fat and KOH, so the reaction happens throughout one solution.
  2. Ethyl acetate is saponified with NaOH. Name the two products.
    Answer
    Sodium acetate (CH₃COO−Na+) and ethanol.

4Acid, base and which layer

Two liquids that do not mix, shaken together in a capped test tube (or a separatory funnel), settle into two layers: here petroleum ether on top and a watery layer below. Each molecule ends up in the layer it likes better, and for a fatty acid that depends on whether it carries a charge.

“Like dissolves like.” Water molecules are polar and hold each other by hydrogen bonds; they surround an ion readily, each one turning its oppositely charged end toward it. Petroleum ether is a mixture of small alkanes (mostly pentanes and hexanes, boiling 30 to 60 °C), with no polarity at all; it dissolves things held together by dispersion forces, like long hydrocarbon chains.

A fatty acid is mostly hydrocarbon. As the neutral acid, RCOOH, its small polar head cannot drag its 17-carbon tail into water, and it goes into the petroleum ether. As the carboxylate, RCOO−, the charged head is surrounded by water so strongly that the ion stays in the water layer (with its tail tucked away in clusters called micelles: this is how soap works).

Which form you have depends on the pH. A carboxylic acid gives up its proton when the solution is more basic than its pKa, and takes one back when it is more acidic:

RCOOH ⇌ RCOO− + H+   pH = pKa + log([RCOO−]/[RCOOH])At a pH one unit below the pKa, ten times more acid than ion; two units below, a hundred times more.

The picture uses pKa = 4.8, typical of a carboxylic acid such as acetic acid. Long-chain fatty acids clumped together in water behave as somewhat weaker acids, which only moves the switch a little higher; it does not change the idea.

Which layer is on top? The less dense one. Petroleum ether (about 0.64 g/mL) floats on water (1.00 g/mL), even with ethanol mixed into the water. In this experiment the layers are in a test tube, and you take the top layer off with a Pasteur pipet, leaving the bottom one behind. Squeeze the bulb before the tip goes in, and draw slowly as the tip nears the boundary.

Two small extractions beat one big one. Each extraction moves a fixed fraction of what is left; two 2 mL portions leave less behind than one 4 mL portion. That is why the procedure extracts the water layer a second time and combines the extracts.

Shaking a capped tube of petroleum ether: it boils near body temperature, so pressure builds as you shake. Hold the cap on, point the tube away from everyone, and loosen the cap briefly to let the pressure out after the first few shakes.

  • Charged (RCOO−) stays in water; neutral (RCOOH) goes into petroleum ether.
  • Well below the pKa the acid form wins; well above it the ion wins.
  • Glycerol, salts and the acid stay in the water layer; petroleum ether is the top layer.

Check yourself

  1. Benzoic acid (pKa 4.2) is dissolved in ether and shaken with aqueous sodium bicarbonate (pH about 8.3). Where is it afterward, and in what form?
    Answer
    In the water, as sodium benzoate: pH 8.3 is four units above its pKa, so it is almost all ion.
  2. You then add acid to that water layer until the pH is 1. What do you see, and why?
    Answer
    Benzoic acid comes back as the neutral acid, which is not very soluble in water: it clouds the solution or comes out as a solid. Shaken with ether, it would go back into the ether.

5Drying a solution

Petroleum ether that has been shaken with water always carries a little water with it, as fine droplets and dissolved. A drying agent takes it out before the solvent is evaporated.

Anhydrous sodium sulfate has no water in its crystals. Given water, it takes it in to form a hydrate, a new crystal with water built into it, up to ten waters per formula unit:

Na₂SO₄ (s, anhydrous) + 10 H₂O → Na₂SO₄·10H₂O (s)Sodium sulfate holds a lot of water but works slowly; give it a few minutes, and swirl.

You can see when you have added enough. While there is water to take up, the grains swell and stick together in clumps at the bottom. Once the water is gone, new grains stay separate and swirl freely, like snow in a snow globe. In this experiment the solution runs through a short column of sodium sulfate in a Pasteur pipet, held up by a small plug of cotton, and the dry solution drips out below into a clean tube.

Dry a wet solution

  • Anhydrous Na₂SO₄ takes water into its crystals as a hydrate; it removes water, nothing else.
  • Enough has been added when fresh grains flow freely instead of clumping.
  • Water left in the extract would end up in the next step, where it matters (topic 6).

Check yourself

  1. Why is the sodium sulfate rinsed with petroleum ether, and the rinse thrown away, before the extract is poured through it?
    Answer
    To wet the bed and wash out any dust or loose fine grains, so that the first drops of the extract are not held back or clouded.
  2. Why is a second 2 mL of petroleum ether poured through after the extract?
    Answer
    The salt and cotton stay wet with extract. Rinsing carries the fatty acids still clinging to them into the tube, so less is lost.

6Making methyl esters

Free fatty acids run badly on silica (topic 7). Turning each one into its methyl ester (a FAME, fatty acid methyl ester) removes the acidic OH. The reaction is a Fischer esterification: acid plus alcohol, with a strong acid as catalyst.

RCOOH + CH₃OH ⇌ RCOOCH₃ + H₂O   (catalyst: H+, here from sulfuric acid)

Methanol is a weak nucleophile and a carboxylic acid’s carbonyl is not very reactive, so on their own they barely react. The acid catalyst protonates the carbonyl oxygen, which makes the carbonyl carbon far more attractive to the methanol. Every step can run backward, and the catalyst is given back at the end.

The mechanism, one step at a time

An equilibrium, and how to push it

Because every step is reversible, the reaction stops at an equilibrium with acid, alcohol, ester and water all present. For a simple acid and alcohol in equal amounts, the equilibrium constant is about 4, and only about two thirds of the acid becomes ester. Le Chatelier’s principle says a system at equilibrium shifts to undo any change you make to it. Try it on a made-up acid and alcohol with K = 4:

K = [ester][water] / ([acid][alcohol]) ≈ 4

Push the equilibrium

Acid turned into ester at equilibrium:

  • Fischer esterification: acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water, catalyzed by a strong acid.
  • Every step is reversible: it stops at an equilibrium, unlike saponification.
  • Adding more of a reactant, or removing a product, shifts it toward the ester.
  • The methyl ester has no acidic O–H, which is what makes it behave on a TLC plate.

Check yourself

  1. Saponification needs a full equivalent of base, but esterification needs only a few drops of acid. Why the difference?
    Answer
    In saponification the hydroxide is consumed (it ends up as water after taking a proton, and the product is a carboxylate). In esterification H+ is given back in the last step, so it is a catalyst and a little goes a long way.
  2. Run the reaction backward: what do you get if you boil methyl acetate with a large excess of water and a little acid?
    Answer
    Acetic acid and methanol: the same equilibrium, pushed the other way by the excess water (acid-catalyzed ester hydrolysis).

Part C

On the plate

Separating the methyl esters, and seeing spots that have no color.

7Thin-layer chromatography

A TLC plate is a thin layer of silica gel on a backing. A solvent creeps up it and carries the spotted compounds with it, each one as far as its grip on the silica allows.

The surface of silica gel is covered in Si–OH groups: it is very polar and makes hydrogen bonds. This is the stationary phase. The mobile phase is the solvent, here hexane with a little diethyl ether (4:1). Every molecule spends its time going back and forth between sticking to the silica and being dissolved in the moving solvent. The more strongly it sticks, the more time it spends standing still, and the shorter the distance it travels.

The retention factor. How far a compound moves depends on how far the solvent went, so it is reported as a fraction:

Rf = (distance from the origin to the center of the spot) ÷ (distance from the origin to the solvent front)always between 0 and 1; no units. On the same plate with the same solvent, the same compound gives the same Rf.

Identifying by comparison. An Rf on its own does not name a compound: it changes with the solvent, the plate and the temperature. So you run standards, known pure compounds, on the same plate beside your sample. A sample spot level with a standard is probably that compound; a spot level with no standard cannot be named this way. Your plate has five lanes: your sample in lanes 1 and 5, and three methyl ester standards in between.

The solvent sets the scale. A more polar mobile phase (more ether) competes better for the silica and moves everything further; a less polar one (more hexane) holds everything back. The best separations have spots between about Rf 0.2 and 0.8. Try it below.

Run a practice plate

Good habits that the procedure asks for, and why:

  • Pencil, never pen, for the origin line and marks: ink is a mixture of dyes and would run up the plate with the solvent.
  • Small spots (about 1 mm): a big spot gives a big smeared spot that overlaps its neighbors.
  • The spots must be above the solvent in the chamber, or they dissolve away into the pool instead of being carried up.
  • Cover the chamber so its air fills with solvent vapor; otherwise the solvent evaporates off the plate as it climbs and the front runs crooked.
  • Mark the front at once when the plate comes out: it disappears as the solvent evaporates within seconds.
  • Hold the plate by its edges, with gloves: your skin is coated with its own oils and fatty acids.

  • Polar silica holds polar molecules back; less polar molecules run further.
  • Rf = spot distance ÷ front distance, both from the origin, both to the center.
  • Name a spot only by comparing it with standards run on the same plate.
  • More polar solvent: every Rf goes up. Less polar: every Rf goes down.

Check yourself

  1. A spot is 3.6 cm from the origin and the front is 8.0 cm from the origin. What is its Rf?
    Answer
    3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45.
  2. Two compounds both run at Rf 0.92 in 1:1 hexane:ether. What would you change?
    Answer
    Use a less polar solvent (more hexane) so that both are held back and spread out in the useful middle of the plate.
  3. A sample spot is level with a standard. Does that prove it is the same compound?
    Answer
    No: different compounds can have the same Rf in one solvent. It is good evidence, made stronger if they still match in a second, different solvent, or by another method (for fatty acid methyl esters, usually gas chromatography).

8Seeing colorless spots: iodine

Methyl esters are colorless, so the developed plate looks blank. Iodine vapor makes the spots show up as yellow-brown patches.

Solid iodine sublimes: it goes straight from solid to a violet vapor without melting. Its vapor dissolves into the organic compounds sitting on the plate much more than into the bare silica, so wherever there is a compound the plate turns brown. The stain is temporary: once the plate is back in the open air the iodine evaporates out of the spots again and they fade, which is why you circle them in pencil straight away.

How much vapor there is depends steeply on the temperature. In this experiment the iodine jar stays at room temperature, closed from the start of the lab so that its air is saturated: there is little vapor, but enough to stain a plate in 5 to 10 minutes. Heating the chamber stains faster, but use the tool to see how much more iodine would then be in the air waiting to come out when it is opened.

Warm the iodine chamber

Vapor pressure from the Clausius–Clapeyron equation with iodine’s enthalpy of sublimation (62.4 kJ/mol) and its vapor pressure at 25 °C (0.041 kPa), NIST.

Iodine vapor irritates the eyes, nose and lungs. Keep the jar closed except to put the plate in and take it out, and open it only in the hood.

  • Iodine sublimes; its vapor dissolves into organic spots and colors them brown.
  • The color fades in air: circle the spots at once.
  • Iodine’s vapor pressure rises steeply with temperature.

Check yourself

  1. Use the tool: roughly how many times more iodine vapor is in the chamber at 110 °C than at 25 °C?
    Answer
    About 250 to 270 times more (about 11 kPa against 0.04 kPa).
  2. A student stains a plate, leaves it on the bench during lunch and comes back to a blank plate. What happened?
    Answer
    The iodine evaporated out of the spots and they faded. The compounds are still there: putting the plate back in the iodine chamber would show them again.

Words to know

Fatty acid
A long-chain carboxylic acid, usually with an even number of carbons (12 to 22).
Saturated / unsaturated
No C=C double bonds / one or more C=C double bonds in the chain.
cis
Both parts of the chain on the same side of a double bond; puts a bend in the chain.
Triglyceride
Glycerol with three fatty acids joined to it as esters; the molecules of fats and oils.
Saponification
Hydrolysis of an ester by a strong base, giving a carboxylate salt (a soap) and an alcohol.
Carboxylate
RCOO−, a carboxylic acid that has lost its proton.
pKa
The pH at which an acid is half in its acid form and half in its ion form.
Extraction
Moving a compound from one liquid into another that does not mix with the first.
Drying agent
An anhydrous salt, such as Na₂SO₄, that removes water from an organic solution by forming a hydrate.
Fischer esterification
Acid-catalyzed reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol to give an ester and water; an equilibrium.
FAME
Fatty acid methyl ester, RCOOCH₃.
Le Chatelier’s principle
A system at equilibrium shifts so as to undo a change made to it.
Stationary / mobile phase
In TLC, the silica gel / the solvent that climbs it.
Rf
Retention factor: distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
Standard
A known pure compound run beside the sample for comparison.
Sublimation
Going straight from solid to gas, as iodine does.
