Part A

The basics

What NMR measures, and the names of a protein’s atoms. Everything later builds on these two topics, so read them even if you think you remember them from organic chemistry.

1What NMR measures

Nuclei, magnets, radio waves, and the four things a spectrum tells you: where a peak is, how it is split, how big it is, and how wide.

Nuclei that behave like tiny magnets

Some atomic nuclei have a property called spin, which makes each of them behave like a tiny bar magnet. The three that matter in this lab, 1H, 13C and 15N, have the simplest kind, spin ½: in a magnetic field each can point only two ways, roughly with the field (a little lower in energy) or against it (a little higher). Not every nucleus will do: ordinary carbon, 12C, has no spin at all and is invisible to NMR, and ordinary nitrogen, 14N, has a spin of 1, which gives very broad, hard-to-use peaks. That is why proteins for NMR are made with 13C and 15N in them (topic 7).

Resonance

The energy gap between the two orientations grows with the strength of the magnetic field, B0. A radio wave whose frequency matches the gap can flip nuclei from one orientation to the other: the nuclei are said to resonate, which is where the “R” in NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) comes from. For protons the frequency is 42.58 MHz for every tesla of field:

ν = (γ / 2π) · B0,   with γ / 2π = 42.58 MHz per tesla for 1H ν: the resonance frequency; B0: the magnet’s field, in tesla; γ: the gyromagnetic ratio, a constant for each kind of nucleus (for 13C about a quarter of the proton’s, for 15N about a tenth)

The magnet in this lab has a field of 28.2 tesla, so its protons resonate at 28.2 × 42.58 ≈ 1200 MHz: it is a “1.2 GHz” spectrometer. Spectrometers are named by their proton frequency, not their field.

A very small majority

At room temperature the two orientations are almost equally populated, because the energy gap is tiny compared with the energy of the molecules’ heat. Even in this lab’s magnet, the excess of nuclei pointing with the field is only about 1 in 10,000, and the whole NMR signal comes from that small excess. This is why NMR is an insensitive method: it needs samples of about a millimolar, where many other methods work with micromolar or less.

Pulse, signal, spectrum

A modern spectrometer does not tune through the frequencies one at a time. It hits the sample with a short, strong pulse of radio waves that tips all the nuclei at once. As they swing back towards the field they spin around it (they precess), each at its own frequency, and the sample gives off a faint radio signal that a coil around it picks up. That signal, a mixture of waves that slowly fade away, is called the free induction decay, or FID. A mathematical operation, the Fourier transform, sorts the mixture into its separate frequencies: each frequency becomes a peak. Recording the whole spectrum in one pulse, and adding up many pulses to beat the noise, is what made modern NMR possible (Richard Ernst, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1991).

1. Where a peak is: the chemical shift

Every nucleus is surrounded by electrons, and the electrons shield it slightly from the magnet’s field. Nuclei in different surroundings therefore feel slightly different fields and resonate at slightly different frequencies. The differences are tiny, a few parts per million of the frequency, so they are given in parts per million (ppm) from a standard compound:

δ (ppm) = (ν − νstandard) / νstandard × 106 For proteins in water the standard is DSS, a water-soluble cousin of the TMS you met in organic chemistry; its methyl protons are 0 ppm.

Because ppm is a ratio, a peak’s shift in ppm is the same on every magnet. The distance between two peaks in hertz, though, grows with the field: that is the first reason for a big magnet (topic 3). The rules from organic chemistry still work: an electronegative neighbour (N, O) pulls electrons away from a proton, deshields it, and moves its peak to a higher shift, further to the left on a spectrum, which by custom runs from high ppm on the left to low ppm on the right.

2. How a peak is split: coupling

Nuclei joined through one, two or three bonds feel each other’s orientation through the electrons of the bonds, and each one’s peak is split into a multiplet (a doublet, a triplet…). The size of the splitting is the coupling constant, J, in hertz. Unlike the chemical shift, J is the same in hertz on any magnet. Some values worth knowing: an amide N–H bond, about 92 Hz; protons on neighbouring carbons (H–C–C–H), 2 to 12 Hz, depending on the angle between the bonds. Couplings matter for more than splitting: through them, magnetization can be passed from one nucleus to its bonded neighbour, which is how most of the experiments in this lab link atoms together.

3. How big a peak is: its area

The area under a peak is proportional to the number of nuclei that give it: the three protons of a methyl give a peak with three times the area of a single proton’s.

4. How wide a peak is: relaxation

After the pulse, the signal fades as the nuclei get out of step with one another; the time it takes is the transverse relaxation time, T2 (its inverse, R2 = 1/T2, is the relaxation rate). A signal that fades fast gives a broad peak:

width at half height (Hz) = 1 / (π T2) = R2 / π

Remember this one: big molecules relax fast, so their peaks are broad, and that is the second reason proteins are hard (topic 4). A real peak is always a little wider than the formula gives, because an uneven magnetic field also makes the signal fade faster; shimming the magnet (topic 3) keeps that extra width small.

  • Nuclei with spin ½ (1H, 13C, 15N) resonate at a frequency set by the field: 42.58 MHz per tesla for protons.
  • A pulse tips all the nuclei at once; the fading signal (the FID) is Fourier-transformed into a spectrum.
  • Shift (ppm): the surroundings; the same on any magnet. Coupling (J, Hz): bonded neighbours. Area: how many nuclei. Width = 1/(πT2): how fast the signal fades.

2A protein, as NMR sees it

The chain, the names of its atoms, the shapes it folds into, and the protein of this lab.

A chain of amino acids

A protein is a chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds: the carboxyl group of one amino acid joined to the amino group of the next, with the loss of water. Each amino acid in the chain is called a residue. The repeating part, N–Cα–C′, is the backbone; each residue’s side chain hangs from its α-carbon and makes it alanine, serine, lysine and so on. Residues are numbered from the end with the free amino group (the N-terminus, residue 1) to the end with the free carboxyl group (the C-terminus).

The names NMR uses

Protein NMR has its own short names for the atoms, and you will meet them on every spectrum:

  • HN (written HN on many spectra): the amide proton, on the backbone nitrogen; N: that nitrogen.
  • Cα and Hα: the α-carbon and its proton. Glycine has two Hα and no side chain.
  • C′ (also written CO): the backbone carbonyl carbon.
  • Cβ, Hβ: the first carbon of the side chain and its protons; then come γ, δ, ε and ζ, going out along the side chain. Two protons on one carbon are numbered (Hβ2, Hβ3).
  • Proline’s side chain bends back and bonds to its own backbone nitrogen, so proline has no HN. The first residue has none either: its nitrogen is a free NH3+ group.

The peptide bond is flat

The C′–N bond has partly the character of a double bond (the nitrogen’s lone pair is shared with the carbonyl), so it cannot turn, and the atoms around it lie in one plane: Cα, C′ and O of one residue, N, HN and Cα of the next. The chain can only turn at the two bonds on either side of each α-carbon (their angles are called φ and ψ). The N–H points one way and the C=O the opposite way, which is what lets the chain hydrogen-bond to itself.

Helices and sheets

  • α-helix: the chain coils into a right-handed spiral with 3.6 residues per turn, rising 1.5 Å per residue (5.4 Å per turn). The C=O of each residue i is hydrogen-bonded to the N–H of residue i + 4, so almost every amide inside a helix is held in a hydrogen bond.
  • β-sheet: stretches of extended chain, the strands, lie side by side, hydrogen-bonded from strand to strand; neighbouring strands run the same way (parallel) or opposite ways (antiparallel).
  • Loops and turns join the helices and strands; they are often at the surface, and often the most mobile parts of a protein.

A backbone hydrogen bond, N–H···O=C, has the nitrogen and the oxygen about 2.8 to 3.0 Å apart, with the N–H pointing roughly at the oxygen. It matters twice in this lab: such amides trade their hydrogen with water slowly (topic 9), and they give the NOEs of helices and sheets their tell-tale patterns.

The fold

Helices and strands pack together into a compact fold, with most of the oily (hydrophobic) side chains buried in a core and most of the charged and polar ones at the surface, in the water. Because each residue’s surroundings in the fold are its own, its nuclei have shifts of their own: this is what lets NMR tell the residues apart, and what is lost when the protein unfolds (topic 8).

Ubiquitin

The protein of this lab is ubiquitin: 76 residues, 8.6 kDa, a sheet of five strands with a helix lying across it. Its name says where it is found: in every cell of plants, animals and fungi. Cells attach it to other proteins, joining its last residue to a lysine side chain of the target, and they build chains of ubiquitins, joined in the same way through lysines of ubiquitin itself. A chain of the right kind marks a protein for destruction by the proteasome, the cell’s shredder (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2004, to Aaron Ciechanover, Avram Hershko and Irwin Rose); other chains, and single ubiquitins, are signals in DNA repair, in moving proteins around the cell and in inflammation, read by the hundreds of proteins that bind ubiquitin. Its sequence has barely changed in a billion years of evolution: yeast’s and ours differ at only 3 of the 76 positions. Small, very stable and easy to make, it is one of the most studied proteins of all, and the standard test sample for new NMR methods.

  • Backbone N–Cα–C′, side chains on Cα; residues numbered from the N-terminus.
  • HN, N, Cα, Hα, C′, Cβ, Hβ: the names of the atoms. Proline and the first residue have no HN; glycine has no Cβ.
  • Helix: C=O of i bonded to N–H of i + 4, 3.6 residues a turn. Sheet: strands bonded side by side.

At the magnet

Why the magnet is so big, why a protein is so hard, how two-dimensional spectra spread the crowd, and how the peaks of a protein are named, counted and put in order.

3Why such a big magnet

The lab’s magnet is one of the strongest NMR magnets in the world. Here is what the strength buys, and what the steps at the magnet are for.

For scale: the Earth’s field is about 0.00005 tesla, a fridge magnet about 0.005, a hospital MRI scanner 1.5 to 3. A teaching NMR in an organic laboratory is typically 7 to 9.4 tesla (300 to 400 MHz). The magnet in this lab is 28.2 tesla, 1.2 GHz. Its coil is a superconductor, which carries its huge current with no resistance as long as it is kept cold, in liquid helium; the 1.2 GHz magnets are kept at about 2 K, colder than ordinary liquid helium’s 4.2 K, by pumping on the helium. The first went into service in 2020. A bigger magnet helps in two ways, both about the crowd of peaks.

  • Spread. Shifts in ppm are the same at any field, but the distance between two peaks in hertz grows with the field, while the width of each peak, in hertz, changes little. So two peaks that overlap on a small magnet stand apart on a big one.
  • Sensitivity. The signal grows faster than the field: in theory roughly as the field to the power 3/2, in practice also depending on the probe and the salt in the sample. A bigger magnet sees a weaker sample, or the same sample in less time. A cryogenically cooled probe, whose coil and first amplifier are cooled to near the temperature of liquid helium to cut their electrical noise, gives about three to five times more signal-to-noise again.

The steps at the magnet, and what each one is for

  • Lock. A magnet’s field drifts very slightly over hours. The sample contains some D2O (usually 5 to 10%, or all of the water in the D2O sample); the spectrometer watches the deuterium’s signal and corrects the field all the time to keep it steady, as a ship’s autopilot holds a course.
  • Shim. Small extra coils around the sample even out the field across its volume. In an uneven field the same proton resonates at slightly different frequencies in different parts of the tube, and every peak is broadened.
  • Tune and match. The probe’s electrical circuit is adjusted to the sample (its salt and water change the circuit), so that the pulses tip the nuclei fully and the faint signal is picked up well.
  • Temperature. Shifts, exchange rates and how fast a protein tumbles all depend on temperature, so it is held constant (the lab uses 25 °C).

  • The lab’s magnet: 28.2 T, 1.2 GHz, a superconducting coil at about 2 K.
  • A bigger field spreads peaks further apart in hertz and gives more signal.
  • Lock holds the field steady, shimming makes it even, tuning matches the probe to the sample.

4Why a protein is hard

Two problems, which grow together with the size of the protein: too many peaks in too little room, and peaks that get broader as the protein gets bigger.

Too many peaks in too little room

Ethyl acetate gives three proton peaks. A protein of 76 residues has about 600 hydrogens, nearly every one in surroundings slightly different from every other’s, and nearly all of their peaks fall between 0 and 10 ppm: on average some 60 peaks in every ppm. In a 1D spectrum they pile on top of one another into a few broad humps (the lab’s first spectrum shows this).

Slow tumbling, broad lines

Molecules in solution tumble about at random. A small molecule turns over in a few picoseconds; a protein, being far larger, takes nanoseconds. The average time a molecule takes to turn through about a radian is its rotational correlation time, τc. The slower a molecule tumbles, the faster its NMR signal fades after the pulse (its R2 grows with τc), and by the rule of topic 1, a signal that fades fast gives a broad peak. So the bigger the protein, the broader its peaks, and the more they run together. A useful rule of thumb for a compact protein in water near room temperature:

τc (ns) ≈ 0.6 × mass (kDa) a rough guide: an elongated or flexible protein, or one in a more viscous or colder solution, tumbles more slowly than the rule says

Broad peaks are also low peaks, since the same area is spread over a wider range, so a large protein’s peaks sink towards the noise. With proton spectra alone, proteins of about 10 kDa can be worked out; with the isotope labelling of topic 7, about 25 to 30 kDa. The ways round that limit are in Session 2’s last part.

Worked example: a made-up 25 kDa protein

  1. τc ≈ 0.6 × 25 = 15 ns.
  2. The lab models the width of an amide proton’s peak on the 1.2 GHz magnet as 1.5 + 1.2 × τc (in Hz, with τc in ns): 1.5 + 1.2 × 15 = 19.5 Hz.
  3. A made-up 5 kDa protein: τc ≈ 3 ns, width 1.5 + 3.6 = 5.1 Hz. The 25 kDa protein’s peaks are 19.5 / 5.1 ≈ 3.8 times broader, and so about 3.8 times lower.

5Reading a 2D spectrum

The way out of the crowd is to spread the peaks over two axes. This topic shows how a 2D spectrum is made and, more important, how to read one.

Why two dimensions help

In a 1D spectrum each peak has one number, its shift, and peaks with nearly the same shift overlap. In a 2D spectrum each peak has two numbers, one on each axis. Two peaks overlap only if both numbers are nearly the same, which happens far less often. The second axis also tells you something the first cannot: which nuclei are linked to which.

How a 2D spectrum is made

A 2D experiment is a long series of 1D experiments, each with four parts (Figure 5.1). The trick is the evolution time, t1, which is made a little longer in each experiment of the series. During t1 the spins precess at their own frequencies, so the signal that is finally recorded rises and falls, from one experiment to the next, at the frequency the spins had before the mixing. Fourier-transforming the recordings gives the second axis, f2; Fourier-transforming along the series, t1, gives the first, f1. A 2D spectrum of a protein takes from minutes to many hours, because the series must be long and every experiment in it repeated to beat the noise. The idea was proposed by Jean Jeener in 1971 and made to work by Richard Ernst’s group.

How to read one

A 2D spectrum is drawn as a map seen from above, each peak a little hill drawn with contour lines. In the experiments of this topic both axes are proton shifts. Read it this way:

  1. The diagonal. Peaks on the diagonal line, where both shifts are equal, are spins that kept their own magnetization through the mixing. The diagonal is simply the 1D spectrum, drawn along a slant.
  2. Cross-peaks. A peak off the diagonal, at f2 = A and f1 = B, says: the spin at shift A and the spin at shift B are linked. Magnetization that started on one ended on the other.
  3. Go to the diagonal. From a cross-peak, go straight up or down to the diagonal and you reach one partner’s own peak; go straight across and you reach the other’s. Cross-peak, partners and the cross-peak’s mirror image on the other side of the diagonal (at B, A) make the four corners of a square.
  4. Read the shifts off the axes, or off the 1D spectra often drawn along the top and the side (the projections).

What counts as “linked” depends on the mixing:

  • COSY (correlation spectroscopy): protons coupled through two or three bonds (H–C–H, H–C–C–H, and the HN–N–Cα–Hα of the backbone): the neighbours you knew from splitting patterns.
  • TOCSY (total correlation spectroscopy): the magnetization is handed along a whole chain of coupled protons during the mixing, so every proton of a connected network links with every other. In a protein each residue’s protons form one such network, its spin system. The chain breaks at every peptide bond, because the carbonyl carbon carries no proton, so spin systems of neighbouring residues never mix.
  • NOESY (nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy): protons close in space, less than about 5 Å apart, bonded or not, in the same residue or in residues far apart in the chain. The effect falls as the distance to the sixth power, so a pair 2.5 Å apart gives a peak 64 times stronger than a pair 5 Å apart.

Common mistakes

  • A NOESY cross-peak does not mean two protons are bonded, and a TOCSY cross-peak does not mean they are close in space.
  • Each cross-peak appears twice, once on each side of the diagonal. Count a link once, not twice.
  • A 2D spectrum gives shifts, not names. That a peak at 4.50 ppm is a serine’s Hα has to be worked out, as the next topics show.

  • Diagonal: the 1D spectrum. Cross-peak at (A, B): A and B are linked; read A straight down or up, B straight across.
  • COSY: 2 or 3 bonds. TOCSY: the whole spin system of a residue. NOESY: through space, under about 5 Å, strength as 1/r6.

    The diagonal peaks at 8.20 and at 4.35 ppm. Its mirror is at f2 = 4.35, f1 = 8.20.
  2. In a TOCSY, the column at one amide proton shows four cross-peaks. What do the four protons have in common?

6Spin systems: naming an amino acid

How the column of a TOCSY at one amide proton tells you what kind of amino acid that residue is.

Cut a TOCSY along the line of one residue’s amide proton and you get a strip: a column of peaks at the shifts of every proton in that residue’s spin system (topic 5), with the amide proton itself on the diagonal. Different amino acids have different numbers of protons, in different places, so the pattern names the amino acid. The method is pattern-matching, the same as in organic chemistry:

  1. Count the peaks other than the diagonal one (a methyl’s three protons give one tall peak; two protons on one carbon often give two peaks).
  2. Look for methyls, tall peaks below 1.5 ppm: none, one or two, and where.
  3. Look between 3.5 and 4.5 ppm for protons other than Hα: they are on a carbon next to an oxygen (serine, threonine).
  4. Look between 2.5 and 3.3 ppm: Hβ next to an aromatic ring or a carboxyl group, or protons next to the nitrogen at the end of a long chain.
  5. Compare the pattern with a table of typical shifts, not one number at a time: every residue’s surroundings in a folded protein are its own, so no residue matches the averages exactly.

7Isotope labelling and the HSQC

How putting 15N and 13C into a protein spreads its peaks over a second, much wider axis, and why the 1H–15N HSQC is the first spectrum anyone records.

Labelling a protein

Only 1.1% of natural carbon is 13C and only 0.37% of natural nitrogen is 15N, far too little to use. So the protein is made with them. The gene for the protein is put into bacteria (usually E. coli), which are grown in a minimal medium, a broth of salts whose only source of nitrogen is 15NH4Cl (about 1 gram per litre) and whose only source of carbon is 13C-glucose (2 to 4 grams per litre). The bacteria build every amino acid from these, so every nitrogen and carbon in the protein they make is 15N and 13C. Labelled glucose is costly, so when 15N alone is enough, as for the HSQC, only the nitrogen is labelled. One NMR sample is about half a millilitre of a 0.2 to 1 mM solution: for a protein of 10 kDa, one to five milligrams of labelled protein.

The HSQC

The 1H–15N HSQC (heteronuclear single-quantum coherence) passes magnetization from each amide proton to the 15N it is bonded to, through their one-bond coupling of about 92 Hz, lets it pick up the nitrogen’s shift during t1, and passes it back to the proton to be recorded. Only protons bonded directly to a 15N take part, so the spectrum has one peak for each N–H, at the proton’s shift on one axis and its nitrogen’s on the other. The amide nitrogens spread over about 100 to 135 ppm, a far wider range than the protons’, which is what pulls the crowd apart. It takes minutes, and it shows at once whether the protein is folded (topic 8), pure and stable; every later experiment hangs on it. It is called the protein’s fingerprint.

Which N–H give peaks

  • The backbone amide of every residue, except the first (its nitrogen is an NH3+ group, whose protons trade places with the water’s too fast to be seen) and every proline (its nitrogen carries no hydrogen).
  • The side-chain NH2 of each asparagine and glutamine: two protons on one nitrogen, so two peaks at the same 15N shift (near 112 ppm), at two different proton shifts (near 6.8 and 7.5 ppm).
  • The ring N–H of each tryptophan, one peak, near 10 ppm 1H and 129 ppm 15N.
  • Arginine’s side-chain N–H give peaks too, but at a 15N shift near 85 ppm, far outside the usual window; they are left out of the counts here.

8Folded or unfolded, and exchange

What an HSQC says about whether a protein is folded, and what happens to peaks when a molecule switches back and forth between two forms.

A folded and an unfolded protein

In a folded protein each N–H sits in surroundings of its own: near an aromatic ring, in a helix, in a hydrogen bond, buried or at the surface. So the HSQC’s peaks spread from about 6 to 10.5 ppm in 1H. Unfold the protein and every residue sees much the same water and much the same floppy chain: the proton shifts crowd into a narrow band, about 8.0 to 8.5 ppm, while the nitrogen shifts, which depend more on the residue’s own kind, still spread. One look at an HSQC tells whether a protein is folded.

Exchange: one molecule, two forms

Suppose each molecule switches between two forms, A and B (folded and unfolded, or free and bound to a partner), with a rate constant k, and that a given nucleus has a different shift in each form, Δν hertz apart. What the spectrum shows depends on how k compares with Δν:

  • Slow exchange, k much smaller than Δν: two separate peaks, one for each form, each as big as that form’s share of the molecules. Folding and unfolding are usually slow like this.
  • Fast exchange, k much larger than Δν: a single peak at the average of the two shifts, weighted by the shares. Many weak bindings are fast, so as a partner is added, a peak slides step by step from its free place to its bound place (topic 12).
  • Intermediate exchange, in between: the peaks broaden, sometimes until they can hardly be seen. For two equally populated forms, the two peaks merge into one, they coalesce, when the rate constant for leaving one form reaches πΔν/√2, about 2.2 times Δν.

Since Δν in hertz grows with the field, the same process can look fast on a small magnet and slow on a big one.

9Hydrogen exchange in D2O

How trading hydrogen for deuterium shows which amides the fold protects, and how to read a half-life.

Hydrogens that trade places with the water

Hydrogens bonded to nitrogen or oxygen are not fixed: they trade places with the hydrogens of the water around them. In D2O they are replaced, one by one, by deuterium, which gives no peak in a 1H spectrum (you used this in organic chemistry to find OH and NH peaks with a “D2O shake”). For a backbone amide in an unfolded chain, the trade is catalysed mainly by hydroxide ion, OH− (OD− in D2O): above about pH 3 it goes ten times faster for every unit of pH, and it is slowest near pH 2.5 to 3. Near neutral pH an exposed amide trades in about a second.

Protected amides

In a folded protein an amide that is buried, or held in a hydrogen bond in a helix or a sheet, cannot trade: the water cannot reach it, or its hydrogen is busy. It trades only when that part of the protein opens up for a moment, by a small local wobble or by unfolding for an instant. Such amides are protected, and may last hours, days or weeks. Usually the protein opens and closes many times before a trade happens, and then the observed rate is the free chain’s rate times the fraction of the time the amide is open. The ratio of the two rates is the protection factor:

P = kintrinsic / kobserved kintrinsic: the rate the same amide would have in an unfolded chain (it can be calculated from the sequence, the pH and the temperature); P of a million means the amide is open about one millionth of the time

What a protected amide looks like in a structure

In a 3D structure, an amide is hydrogen-bonded when its N–H points at a carbonyl oxygen with the N and the O within about 3.5 Å (usually 2.8 to 3.0). In a helix, its partner is the C=O four residues back; in a sheet, a C=O on the neighbouring strand. Amides at the surface whose N–H points into the water are not protected.

The experiment

The protein is dissolved in D2O and HSQCs are recorded one after another, over minutes to weeks. Each amide’s peak fades as its hydrogen is replaced:

height(t) = height(0) · e−kt,   half-life t½ = ln 2 / k k: the observed exchange rate constant; read t½ where the height has fallen to half of its starting height

The first spectrum takes some minutes, so amides that trade faster than that are gone before it is recorded: only protected ones are seen at all. The ones that last longest are the protein’s most stable hydrogen bonds. Because the half-lives run from minutes to weeks, they are plotted against time on a log scale, where each step of the axis is ten times the one before.

10The sequential walk

To use any of the peaks, each must be given to its residue. This is called assignment, and it is done by walking along the chain.

The old way: NOEs

Kurt Wüthrich’s method (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2002; his group’s structure of a small proteinase inhibitor, in 1985, was among the very first protein structures solved by NMR) used proton spectra alone. First, a TOCSY finds every spin system and its kind of amino acid (topic 6). Then a NOESY finds which spin system comes next in the chain, because the HN of each residue is usually close in space to the Hα of the residue before it (2.2 to 3.6 Å, depending on the backbone’s shape), and, in a helix, to the HN before it. Matching the spin systems’ kinds to the sequence fixes where each stretch belongs. It works, but slowly: in a crowd, many amide protons have almost the same shift, and each NOE could belong to several of them.

The modern way: triple resonance

With 13C and 15N in the protein, triple-resonance experiments (1H, 13C and 15N) pass magnetization along the bonds of the backbone itself, through its couplings, which are large and do not depend on the protein’s shape. They are 3D experiments, with a 1H, a 15N and a 13C axis; they are looked at as strips: thin slices cut at each HSQC peak’s 1H and 15N, showing the 13C axis.

The HNCACB starts on each HN, goes to its N, and from the N to two Cα: its own residue’s, through a one-bond coupling of about 10 Hz, and the previous residue’s, through a two-bond coupling of about 7 Hz. From each Cα it reaches that residue’s Cβ (a one-bond coupling of about 35 Hz), and it comes back the same way. So each strip shows four peaks: its own residue’s Cα and Cβ, strong (the larger coupling), and the previous residue’s Cα and Cβ, weak. Cα and Cβ peaks have opposite signs, so they are drawn in different colours.

11From NOEs to a structure

Once every peak has its residue, the NOESY becomes a list of distances, and a computer folds a chain to fit them.

Restraints

After assignment (topic 10), each NOESY cross-peak can be read as: this proton of residue i is within so many ångströms of that proton of residue j. Such a statement is a restraint. Because a peak’s height falls as 1/r6 (topic 5), but depends on other things too, peaks are not turned into exact distances; they are sorted into classes, typically:

Other restraints add the backbone’s angles: the coupling between HN and Hα (about 4 Hz in a helix, about 9 Hz in a strand, through the Karplus relation you may remember from organic chemistry) and the chemical shifts themselves, since Cα, Cβ and Hα shifts differ between helices and strands. Amides that exchange slowly in D2O (topic 9) can add hydrogen-bond restraints. A small protein ends up with one to two thousand restraints, or more.

Short-range and long-range NOEs

NOEs are sorted by how far apart in the sequence the two residues are:

  • Within a residue, and sequential (residues i and i + 1): they say little about the fold, but they are the ones that assign (topic 10).
  • Medium range (i to i + 2, 3 or 4): the signature of a helix, whose turns bring residue i near i + 3 and i + 4.
  • Long range (five or more residues apart): two parts of the chain that are far apart in the sequence but touch in the fold, like two strands lying side by side in a sheet, or a helix packed on a sheet. These are the ones that fix the fold.

The calculation, and the bundle

A program (CYANA and XPLOR-NIH are two widely used) starts from a chain of the right sequence in a random shape. It then “heats” the model, letting it move freely, and cools it slowly while the restraints pull it into shape, a method called simulated annealing after the way metal is annealed. It does this many times, from many random starts, and keeps the solutions that break the fewest restraints with sensible bond lengths and angles: usually 20 of them (anything from 10 to 20 is common), deposited together as the structure. Drawn on top of each other they make a bundle.

Where the restraints are many, as in a well-packed core, the models agree closely; where they are few, as in loops and the ends of the chain, they spread. The spread says how well each part is defined by the data, which is not the same as how much it moves: a part may spread because it moves, or only because few NOEs reached it. Special ensembles can be calculated to show motion, from measurements of how the protein moves.

12Binding, seen by NMR

Add a partner to a labelled protein, record an HSQC after each addition, and the peaks that move show where the partner touches.

How tightly: Kd

A protein P and its partner L bind and come apart, P + L ⇌ PL. The dissociation constant, Kd = [P][L] / [PL], is the concentration of free partner at which half the protein is bound: the smaller Kd, the tighter the binding. For a binding of Kd = 10 µM, adding partner equal to the protein, both at 200 µM, binds about 80% of the protein; tighter bindings bind nearly all of it. The exact share bound comes from a quadratic equation, the one the lab uses:

fraction bound = [ (Pt + Lt + Kd) − √( (Pt + Lt + Kd)² − 4 Pt Lt ) ] / 2Pt Pt, Lt: the total concentrations of protein and partner, in the same units as Kd

A titration

The partner is added in steps, often from a fifth of the protein’s amount up to two or three times it, with an HSQC after each step. Wherever the partner touches, the surroundings of the N–H change, and so do their shifts: this is chemical shift perturbation. How the peaks change depends on exchange (topic 8):

  • Fast exchange, common for weak bindings (Kd in the micromolar range or weaker): each peak slides in a straight line from its free place towards its bound place, as far along as the share bound.
  • Slow exchange, common for tight bindings: the free peak shrinks while a new, bound peak grows somewhere else.
  • Intermediate: the moving peaks broaden and may vanish part way through.

How far a peak moves

A peak moves in two directions at once, in 1H and in 15N. The two are combined into one number, the nitrogen’s change being scaled down because nitrogen shifts spread over a range several times wider than protons’:

Δδ = √( ΔδH² + (0.14 · ΔδN)² ) ΔδH, ΔδN: the change in each shift, in ppm, between free and fully bound

Coloured onto the structure, the residues that move most mark the binding face. A word of caution: a residue may also move because the binding nudges the protein’s shape a little further away, so the map shows where the binding is felt, which is mostly but not only where it touches.

13Crystals and diffraction

X-ray crystallography sees electrons, not nuclei, and needs a crystal. How a crystal is grown, and how it turns X-rays into spots.

Why a crystal

A single molecule scatters X-rays far too weakly to measure. In a crystal, millions of millions of molecules sit in the same orientation, repeated in three dimensions, and their scattered waves add up, but only in particular directions. The repeating block of a crystal is its unit cell. A protein crystal is unlike salt or sugar: it is about half water (from about a quarter to three quarters, depending on the crystal), with the molecules touching at only a few points. It is soft and fragile, and dies if it dries out.

Growing one

The protein is pushed slowly out of solution, so slowly that its molecules have time to pack in order instead of clumping. The usual way is vapour diffusion (Figure 13.1): a drop of protein solution mixed with a precipitant (a salt, a polymer such as polyethylene glycol, a buffer) is sealed in a small well above a reservoir of the same precipitant, more concentrated. Water slowly leaves the drop for the reservoir, so the protein and precipitant in the drop slowly concentrate, until the protein is supersaturated: a few crystals form (nucleation) and grow over days to weeks. Nobody can predict which mixture will work, so hundreds are tried: a robot sets drops of a few hundred nanolitres each, and an imaging incubator photographs them for weeks.

Harvesting, and keeping it cold

A good crystal is scooped out of its drop in a tiny nylon loop, dipped for a moment in a cryoprotectant (the drop’s solution with glycerol or a similar compound added, so that the water freezes as a glass and not as ice crystals, which would wreck the pattern), and plunged into liquid nitrogen. On the diffractometer it sits in a stream of nitrogen gas at about 100 K. Frozen, it survives the X-ray beam for many minutes to hours; at room temperature the beam’s damage would ruin it far sooner.

X-rays

X-rays for diffraction have wavelengths of about an ångström, close to the distances between atoms. A laboratory source makes them by firing electrons at a metal target: copper gives its Kα line at 1.5418 Å; the newer liquid-metal-jet sources, like the one in this lab’s X-ray room, use a jet of a gallium alloy and give gallium’s Kα line, 1.34 Å. Synchrotrons, particle accelerators built as light sources, give beams millions of times brighter, at any wavelength chosen.

Diffraction and Bragg’s law

The crystal’s regular arrangement can be pictured as sets of parallel planes, a distance d apart. X-rays reflected from one plane and from the next add up only when the extra path of the second, 2d sin θ, is a whole number of wavelengths:

λ = 2d sin θ  (Bragg’s law) λ: the X-rays’ wavelength; d: the spacing of the planes; θ: the angle between the beam and the planes, so that the spot lies at 2θ from the straight-through beam. Worked out by Lawrence Bragg in 1912; he and his father, William Bragg, shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1915.

On a flat detector a distance D from the crystal, a spot a distance r from the beam’s centre lies at an angle given by tan 2θ = r / D. Finely spaced planes give spots far from the centre. The smallest spacing d whose spots can still be measured is the resolution: the finer it is (the smaller the number), the more detail the structure shows. To measure every spot, the crystal is turned in small steps, a fraction of a degree each, and a picture, a frame, is taken at each step.

  3. Which were the first protein structures solved by X-ray crystallography, and when?
    Show the answer
    Myoglobin, by John Kendrew’s group (1958, at low resolution; at 2 Å in 1960), and haemoglobin, by Max Perutz’s (1960). They shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1962.

14Phases, maps and B-factors

How the spots become a picture of the electrons, what resolution means in that picture, and what a B-factor says about each atom.

The phase problem

Each spot is a scattered wave, and to turn the waves back into a picture of the molecule two things are needed for each: how strong it is and when its crests arrive, its phase. The detector measures only the strength. The phases are lost, and must be found another way:

  • Molecular replacement: borrow them from a similar structure already known, or, today, from a predicted one (topic 15). This is how most new structures are phased.
  • Anomalous scattering (SAD and MAD): grow the protein with selenomethionine in place of methionine, or soak in heavy atoms, and use the way those atoms scatter X-rays of chosen wavelengths.
  • Isomorphous replacement: compare the spots of crystals with and without heavy atoms bound (the method of the first protein structures).

The map, and the model

With phases, the spots are turned into an electron density map: a 3D cloud showing where the electrons are, drawn as a mesh at one chosen level, like a single contour on a map of hills. The crystallographer builds the chain into it, residue by residue, then refines the model: the computer moves the atoms to make the spots the model would give match the measured ones. Two numbers say how well they match: R, over the spots used in refining, and R-free, over a test set of 5 to 10% of the spots that were left out on purpose, so that it cannot be improved by cheating. At about 2 Å, a well-refined structure has an R of about 0.16 to 0.20 and an R-free of about 0.20 to 0.25.

What each resolution shows

  • At about 3.5 Å the chain is a tube and the side chains are lumps.
  • At about 2.5 Å the side chains take their shapes.
  • At about 1.5 Å or better, atoms begin to separate, and a ring shows the hole in its middle.

Hydrogens, with one electron each, barely show except at the very finest resolutions, around 1 Å. This is one place where NMR, built on hydrogens, sees what X-rays miss.

The B-factor

Each atom of a crystal structure carries a B-factor, which says how smeared out it is, by vibration and by small differences between the molecules of the crystal:

B = 8π² ⟨u²⟩ ⟨u²⟩: the atom’s mean square displacement in any one direction, in Å². B = 20 Å² means it wanders about 0.5 Å (root mean square) along any one direction.

Low B means well ordered: atoms of the packed core often have B of 10 to 30 Å². Loops and chain ends at the surface usually have higher B. Structures are often coloured by B, blue for low, red for high.

A made-up ring at different resolutions

The density of a made-up flat ring of six atoms with one atom attached, as a map at the chosen resolution would show it. Find where the hole appears.

  • Spots give strengths, not phases; phases come from a similar or predicted structure, or from heavy or anomalous atoms.
  • R and R-free say how well the model matches the spots; R-free uses spots left out of refining.
  • 3.5 Å: a tube. 2.5 Å: side chains. 1.5 Å: holes in rings. B = 8π²⟨u²⟩: low is ordered.

Check yourself

  1. An atom has B = 79 Å². About how far does it wander, in any one direction?
    Show the answer
    ⟨u²⟩ = 79 / (8π²) = 79 / 79 = 1.0 Å², so about 1 Å (root mean square).
  2. Why is R-free a more honest number than R?
    Show the answer
    The model was adjusted to fit the spots in R, so R can be made small by over-fitting. The spots in R-free were never used to adjust the model, so they test it fairly.
  3. At which resolution could you tell a phenylalanine’s ring from a leucine’s side chain by its shape?
    Show the answer
    From about 2.5 Å the shapes differ (flat and wide against branched); at 3.5 Å both are lumps.

15Prediction, and comparing structures

Structures can now be predicted from the sequence alone. How the predictions are judged, and how any two structures are compared.

AlphaFold

AlphaFold, a program from Google DeepMind, predicts a protein’s structure from its sequence, having learned from the structures solved by X-ray, NMR and electron microscopy. Its second version stood out at the 2020 round of CASP, the blind test of structure prediction, and was published in 2021; its database now holds predictions for more than 200 million proteins. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it; the other half went to David Baker, for designing new proteins by computer.

Each residue of a prediction comes with AlphaFold’s own confidence, the pLDDT, from 0 to 100: above 90, very high; 70 to 90, confident; 50 to 70, low; below 50, very low, which often marks a part of the chain that has no fixed structure at all. Predictions are weakest at what a single structure cannot show: how a protein moves, the different shapes it takes, and how a partner changes it.

Electron microscopy

Cryo-electron microscopy (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2017, to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson) freezes the molecules in a thin layer of glassy ice, photographs tens of thousands to millions of them with electrons, and averages the images. It needs no crystal and suits large complexes; for the most favourable samples it now reaches about 1.2 Å.

Comparing two structures: the RMSD

To compare two structures of the same protein, one is laid on the other: the computer finds the turn and shift that bring matching atoms (usually the Cα atoms) as close together as possible. Then the distance between each pair of matching atoms is measured, and the distances are combined into the root mean square deviation:

RMSD = √( (d1² + d2² + … + dN²) / N ) di: the distance between the i-th pair of matching atoms, after laying one structure on the other; N: the number of pairs

Worked example: four made-up atoms

After laying two made-up structures on each other, four matching Cα atoms are 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 and 0.6 Å apart:

RMSD = √( (0.09 + 0.16 + 0.25 + 0.36) / 4 ) = √0.215 = 0.46 Å.

Now suppose the fourth atom sits on a floppy end of the chain and is 3.0 Å away instead: RMSD = √( (0.09 + 0.16 + 0.25 + 9.00) / 4 ) = √2.375 = 1.54 Å. One atom more than tripled it. Because distances are squared, a few far-off atoms dominate an RMSD, and this is why structures are often compared over their well-ordered core only.

As a rough guide for Cα atoms: under about 0.5 Å, the two are the same within the error of the methods; 1 to 2 Å, the same fold with small differences; above 3 to 4 Å, real differences in shape. Where an experimental structure and a prediction differ, it is often where the protein moves, or where its surroundings (the neighbours in a crystal, a partner, the rest of a longer chain) shape it.

  • AlphaFold predicts a structure from a sequence, with a confidence (pLDDT) for each residue.
  • Cryo-EM averages images of frozen molecules; no crystal needed.
  • RMSD: the root mean square of the distances between matching atoms after superposition; a few far-off atoms dominate it.

Check yourself

  1. Three matching atoms are 1, 1 and 2 Å apart. What is the RMSD?
    Show the answer
    √( (1 + 1 + 4) / 3 ) = √2 = 1.41 Å.
  2. A prediction’s last ten residues have pLDDT below 50. What does that suggest?
    Show the answer
    That AlphaFold has little confidence there; very low values often mark a part of the chain with no fixed structure, which may well be flexible in the real protein.

16Safety at the magnet and the X-ray cabinet

The lab’s rooms are simulated, but these are the rules of the real ones.

The field of a superconducting magnet is always on: it cannot be switched off for a visit. Its stray field reaches well outside the magnet’s case, and the bigger the magnet, the further.

  • The 5 gauss line, marked on the floor, is where the stray field falls to 0.5 millitesla. People with a pacemaker or another implanted medical device must stay outside it unless the hall’s safety officer has checked the device and cleared it.
  • Steel is pulled in, harder the closer it gets: tools, gas cylinders, chairs, even hair pins can fly at the magnet and hurt anyone in the way. Nothing magnetic goes inside the line. Watches, phones and cards with magnetic stripes can be damaged.
  • Cryogens. The coil is kept superconducting by liquid helium (in the 1.2 GHz magnets at about 2 K), inside a jacket usually cooled with liquid nitrogen at 77 K. Filling them is done by trained staff, with face shields and cryogenic gloves.
  • A quench, the sudden loss of superconductivity, boils off the helium in seconds. It is piped out of the room through the quench line, but a leak could push the air’s oxygen out of the room. That is why the hall has an oxygen monitor, and why everyone leaves at once when it sounds.
  • X-rays are made only inside the diffractometer’s shielded cabinet, and only with its doors shut: an interlock keeps the doors locked while the X-rays are on, and lamps on the cabinet show it. Never try to defeat an interlock.
  • Liquid nitrogen for freezing crystals: face shield and cryogenic gloves. It can burn skin in seconds, and in a closed room its gas can push out the oxygen.

17Words to know

In alphabetical order. The number after each says where it is explained.

Assignment
Giving each peak its atom in its residue. (10)
B-factor
How smeared out an atom is in a crystal structure: B = 8π²⟨u²⟩; low, well ordered; high, mobile or disordered. (14)
Bundle
The set of models, usually 20, that best satisfy an NMR structure’s restraints. (11)
Chemical shift (δ)
Where a nucleus resonates, in parts per million from a standard (DSS for proteins); the same on any magnet. (1)
Chemical shift perturbation
The movement of peaks when a partner binds; Δδ = √(ΔδH² + (0.14 ΔδN)²). (12)
Coalescence
When two exchanging peaks merge into one, at k = πΔν/√2 for two equal forms. (8)
COSY
A 2D spectrum linking protons coupled through two or three bonds. (5)
Coupling (J)
The interaction between nuclei through bonds, in hertz; splits peaks and carries magnetization from atom to atom. (1)
Cross-peak
A peak off the diagonal of a 2D spectrum: the two shifts it lies at are linked. (5)
Cryoprobe
A probe whose coil and first amplifier are cooled to cut their noise, giving three to five times more signal-to-noise. (3)
Diagonal
The line of a homonuclear 2D spectrum where both shifts are equal; it holds the 1D spectrum. (5)
Electron density map
The 3D picture of where the electrons are, made from the spots and their phases. (14)
Exchange (slow, fast)
Switching between two forms slower than the peaks’ separation in hertz (two peaks) or faster (one averaged peak). (8)
FID
Free induction decay: the fading signal recorded after a pulse, Fourier-transformed into a spectrum. (1)
Fold
The compact 3D shape a protein’s chain takes. (2)
Frame
One picture of the diffraction, taken while the crystal turns a fraction of a degree. (13)
HN, Hα, Cα, Cβ, C′
The amide proton, the α-proton and α-carbon, the first side-chain carbon, the carbonyl carbon. (2)
HNCACB
A 3D experiment linking each amide to the Cα and Cβ of its own residue (strong) and of the one before (weak). (10)
HSQC
A 2D spectrum with one peak for each H bonded to a 15N (or a 13C); the protein’s fingerprint. (7)
Kd
The dissociation constant: the free partner concentration at which half the protein is bound. (12)
Lock, shim, tune
Holding the field steady on the deuterium signal; evening out the field across the sample; matching the probe to the sample. (3)
Long-range NOE
An NOE between residues five or more apart in the sequence; these fix the fold. (11)
NOE, NOESY
The nuclear Overhauser effect: transfer between protons close in space (under about 5 Å), falling as 1/r6; the 2D spectrum that shows it. (5)
Phase problem
The detector measures how strong each scattered wave is but not its phase, which must be found another way. (14)
pLDDT
AlphaFold’s confidence in each residue of a prediction, 0 to 100. (15)
Protection factor
How many times slower an amide trades its hydrogen in the folded protein than in a free chain. (9)
R, R-free
How well a crystal structure’s model matches the measured spots; R-free over spots left out of refining. (14)
Relaxation (T2, R2)
How fast the signal fades; width at half height = 1/(πT2). (1)
Residue
One amino acid in a protein chain. (2)
Resolution
The finest spacing measured in the diffraction, and so the finest detail the map can show; smaller is better. (13)
Restraint
A distance or angle a structure must satisfy, from an NOE, a coupling or a shift. (11)
RMSD
Root mean square deviation: how far apart matching atoms of two structures lie, after laying one on the other. (15)
Spin system
The protons of one residue, linked by couplings; seen together in a TOCSY. (5, 6)
Strip
A thin slice of a 2D or 3D spectrum at one amide’s shift(s). (6, 10)
τc
The rotational correlation time: how long a molecule takes to tumble; about 0.6 ns per kDa for a compact protein. (4)
TOCSY
A 2D spectrum linking every proton of a spin system with every other. (5)
Unit cell
The block that repeats to build a crystal. (13)
Vapour diffusion
Growing crystals in a drop that slowly loses water to a reservoir. (13)
