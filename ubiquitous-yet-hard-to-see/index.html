            2The 1D proton spectrum

            Every one of ubiquitin's 600-odd hydrogens gives a signal. In a small molecule each would stand alone; here they pile up. Worse, a protein tumbles slowly in water, and the slower a molecule tumbles, the faster its signal dies away and the broader its lines. A rule of thumb for a globular protein near room temperature: the tumbling time, τc, is about 0.6 ns for every kDa of mass (ubiquitin, 8.6 kDa, measures 4.1 ns). Use the slider to see a bigger protein: the page draws ubiquitin's protons copied as many times as the mass asks, with the lines its slower tumbling gives.

            3The homonuclear way: TOCSY

            Before isotope labelling, proteins were worked out from proton spectra alone (Kurt Wüthrich’s method, Nobel Prize 2002). A TOCSY passes magnetization along the chain of coupled protons inside one amino acid, so a column of the 2D spectrum at one residue’s amide proton shows every proton of its side chain: its spin system, a fingerprint of the amino acid.             4NOESY, and the crowd

            A NOESY shows a peak between two protons that are close in space (under about 5 Å), whether or not they are in the same residue, with heights falling off as the distance to the sixth power. From an amide proton, the strongest are often to the residue before: that is how Wüthrich walked along the chain. The trouble is telling which amide a column belongs to: in a protein many amide protons have almost the same shift.

            6Folded or unfolded?

            A folded protein’s amide peaks spread widely, because each N–H sits in its own surroundings. Unfold it and every residue sees much the same water around it: the peaks crowd into a narrow band near 8.0 to 8.5 ppm. Ubiquitin is so stable that it unfolds only in acid with 8 M urea. Unfolding and folding are slow on the NMR time scale, so a sample that is partly unfolded shows both sets of peaks at once, each as tall as its share. Sample B is your mystery: click a peak to read its height, where a peak of the fully folded protein would be 1.00.             7The D2O shake

            As in the organic unknown lab, D2O trades deuterium for the hydrogens on N and O, and a deuterium gives no peak in a 1H spectrum. An amide at the surface trades in seconds; one held in a hydrogen bond deep in the fold may wait for weeks, because it trades only when the protein opens up for a moment.             8A short sequential walk

            With 13C and 15N in the protein, the HNCACB links each amide to the carbons around it.             2From NOEs to a structure

            A structure calculation starts from a list of restraints: thousands of NOEs, each saying that two protons are within about 5 Å of each other (stronger peaks, closer), with angles from the shifts and couplings. A computer folds a chain until it satisfies them all, many times over from different starts; the best few dozen solutions are kept as the structure, a bundle of models. Where the restraints are many, the models agree; where they are few, they spread. Below: your residue’s own amide NOEs drawn as lines on the NMR structure (PDB 1D3Z).

            3Binding: where does a partner touch?

            4Growing a crystal

            5Diffraction and resolution

            X-rays scatter from the electrons of every atom; the crystal’s order makes the scattered waves add up only in certain directions, as spots on the detector (Bragg’s law: a family of planes d apart gives spots at an angle 2θ from the beam where λ = 2d sin θ). Spots far from the middle come from finely spaced planes, so the furthest spots set the resolution: the finest detail the map can show. The home machine’s source gives gallium Kα X-rays, λ = 1.34 Å.

            6The electron density

            The spots give the strength of each scattered wave but not its phase (when its crests arrive); the phases have to be found another way (from a known similar structure, or from heavy atoms), and with them the spots turn into a map of the electron density, into which the atoms are built. Hydrogens, with one electron each, barely show.             How well ordered? The B-factor

            Each atom in a crystal structure carries a B-factor: how much it is smeared out, by vibration and by small differences from one molecule of the crystal to the next. Low B, well ordered; high B, mobile or disordered.

            8Where the field is now

            Bigger proteins

            Proton spectra alone reach proteins of about 10 kDa; with 13C and 15N, about 25 to 30 kDa. TROSY (Pervushin, Riek, Wider and Wüthrich, 1997) keeps the one line of each N–H whose two relaxation mechanisms cancel, so it stays narrow in a slowly tumbling protein; with deuterium in place of most hydrogens, 100 kDa and more. Labelling only the methyls of Ile, Leu and Val in an otherwise deuterated protein (Lewis Kay’s group) reaches complexes of up to about a megadalton, such as the 670 kDa proteasome core (2007), though not full structures.

            More signal, less time

            Cryogenically cooled probes give about 3 to 5 times the signal-to-noise, cutting measuring time 10 to 25 fold. The first 1.2 GHz magnet went into service in Florence in 2020; a 1.3 GHz magnet was shown by its maker in 2025. Non-uniform sampling records only part of a 3D or 4D experiment and fills in the rest, turning days into hours.

            Computers do the assignment

            Programs now assign the peaks automatically, and backbone angles, even whole folds, can be worked out from chemical shifts alone (TALOS-N, CS-Rosetta).

            What NMR sees that nothing else does: motion

            Relaxation dispersion and CEST detect forms of a protein present only 1% of the time, lasting a millisecond to tens of milliseconds: the shapes it passes through as it binds, folds or works. NMR also works inside living cells (ubiquitin’s amides were found to exchange much faster in human cells than in the test tube, 2009) and, with spinning solid samples, on amyloid fibrils and membrane proteins.

            Cryo-EM and AlphaFold

            Electron microscopy of frozen samples (Nobel Prize 2017: Dubochet, Frank and Henderson) now solves large complexes that never crystallise; it is catching up with X-ray: in 2025 the PDB released about 7,200 cryo-EM structures against about 10,100 X-ray ones and 230 by solution NMR. AlphaFold (Nobel Prize 2024 to Hassabis and Jumper, with Baker for protein design) predicts a structure from the sequence, as you saw above, often within an ångström of the crystal. So today NMR and X-ray are used less to find a fold for the first time, and more for what prediction cannot give: how a protein moves, what it binds and where, the parts that are disordered, and checking the predictions themselves.
