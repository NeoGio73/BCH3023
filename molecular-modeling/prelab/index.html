1What you are looking at

Glucagon is a hormone of 29 amino acids. The α-cells of the pancreas release it when blood glucose falls, and it tells the liver to release glucose. The structure in the viewer was worked out by X-ray crystallography in 1975 (Sasaki and co-workers, Nature 257, 751), from glucagon of the pig, and is kept in the Protein Data Bank as entry 1GCN.

A structure is a list of atoms and where each one is, in ångströms (1 Å = 0.1 nm), along three axes, x, y and z. The viewer draws the atoms in their colors: carbon grey, nitrogen blue, oxygen red, sulfur yellow. Hydrogens are missing: X-rays are scattered by electrons, and a hydrogen has only one, so its position is rarely found.

Try it. Drag to turn the molecule, scroll or pinch to zoom, and point at an atom (or tap it): its residue, its name and its number in the file appear under the viewer. Try each Draw as style. Cartoon hides the atoms and shows the chain's path.

2Views and axes

3The two ends of the chain

The N-terminus is the end with a free amino group: a nitrogen bonded to an α-carbon and to nothing else in the chain. The C-terminus is the end with a free carboxyl group: a carbon bonded to an α-carbon and to two oxygens. Find both ends, and the first and last three residues.

The N-terminus is His 1: its nitrogen (atom N, the first atom in the file) is bonded only to the α-carbon. The C-terminus is Thr 29: its carbon C carries two oxygens, O and OXT ("terminal oxygen"), which only the last residue has.

First three residues, N to C: His – Ser – Gln. Last three: Met – Asn – Thr. A sequence is always written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

5Helices, strands and disulfides

        
Draw glucagon as a Cartoon. Read its HELIX lines: how many helices, and where does the longest start and end? Does it have β-strands (SHEET lines) or disulfide bonds (SSBOND lines)?

        
        

          
One HELIX line: a single α-helix from Phe 6 to Leu 26, 21 residues. There are no SHEET lines (no β-strands) and no SSBOND lines (glucagon has no cysteine, so it can have no disulfide bond).

          

          
Looking down the helix, the chain winds round an open core, 3.6 residues to a turn, with the side chains pointing outward. Glucagon is only partly helical in solution; in the crystal it packs as a helix.

        

        

        
The worksheet's "fuzzy caterpillar" is Trp 25 drawn thick: its two flat rings stand out from the helix.

      


      

        
6Side chains

        
Press Show: Acidic, then Basic, then Aromatic. How many of each, and which aromatic residue has the largest side chain?

        
        

          
Acidic: 3, Asp 9, Asp 15 and Asp 21 (no glutamate). Basic: 4, His 1, Lys 12, Arg 17 and Arg 18. Aromatic: 5, Phe 6, Tyr 10, Tyr 13, Phe 22 and Trp 25. The largest aromatic side chain is tryptophan's, with its two fused rings (an indole).

          
This lab counts histidine as basic, as the worksheet and Jmol do. At pH 7 most histidine side chains are in fact uncharged: its pKa is near 6.

        

      


      

        
7An angle at an α-carbon

        
Measure the N–Cα–C angle of Phe 6: press Measure: Angle and click its N, its CA and its C, in that order (the angle is at the second atom).

        

        
        

          
111.1°. The α-carbon is sp³, so you might expect 109.5°, the tetrahedral angle. The angle in proteins is usually a little larger, about 111°, because the four groups on the α-carbon (N, C, the side chain and H) are not alike and the chain pulls on it. In glucagon the N–Cα–C angles run from 109° to 124°; this structure was solved at 3.0 Å, less exactly than most, so its angles vary more.

          
Measure a distance too: the N–CA bond is about 1.46 Å, the C=O bond about 1.23 Å.

9What protein is this? Using BLAST

        
Glucagon's file names it (COMPND) and says where it came from (SOURCE: Sus scrofa, the pig). Your unknown's file will not, so you will search its sequence with BLAST, which lines it up against every protein sequence known and lists the closest.

        

          BLAST glucagon at NCBI
          BLAST at UniProt
        

        
        

          
Searched against UniProt's reviewed proteins (Swiss-Prot), glucagon's sequence finds glucagon at the top, at 100% identity over all 29 residues, from the squirrel monkey, the rabbit and the camel, then pro-glucagon from the rat and the sheep, also at 100%. Turkey and opossum glucagon follow at 96.6% (one residue different), and those of the duck, the ostrich and the alligator at 93.1% (two different).

          
Two lessons for your unknown:

          
1. Read the identity, not only the top line. 100% over the whole sequence names the protein; 95% or less may be a relative.

          
2. The same sequence can belong to many species. Glucagon is identical in nearly all mammals, the pig and humans included, so the sequence alone cannot say which mammal this one came from. If your best hits are the same protein from several organisms at 100%, say so.

          
Some hits say pro-glucagon: glucagon is cut from a longer precursor. The intestine cuts the same precursor differently to make GLP-1, the hormone that drugs such as semaglutide imitate. Your unknown, too, may be listed under its precursor's name.
