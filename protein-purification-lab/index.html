Setting up the bench

Bench clock 0:00
µL (10 to 1000 µL: every pipetful you add uses this volume)

A. Break the cells open

  1. Lysis buffer onto the pellet (4 mL) The pellet is taken up in about 4 mL. The buffer's 10 mM imidazole keeps weakly sticky bacterial proteins off the column later.
  2. Resuspend the pellet Pipet up and down until no lumps are left, so every cell meets the buffer.
  3. Keep a sample: whole cells (20 µL)
  4. Lysozyme (1 mg/mL in the tube, from the 10 mg/mL stock) Lysozyme digests the cell wall. Work out the volume, set the micropipette to it, then add it.
  5. 30 minutes on ice The bench clock runs on its own; "Speed up the wait" makes it run 60 times faster.
  6. Sonicate: six bursts of 10 s, 10 s on ice between them The probe shakes the liquid with ultrasound and breaks the softened cells open. Each burst warms the sample: watch its temperature on the sonicator's screen ("Sonicator screen" under the room shows it close up).
  7. Keep a sample: lysate

B. Clear the lysate

  1. Pour the lysate into the spin tube
  2. Make a balance tube Water in the balance tube, to the same volume as your lysate. A centrifuge spun unbalanced shakes itself apart, so it refuses to start.
  3. Both tubes into the rotor, opposite each other
  4. Close the lid, set the speed and the time, start The rotor's radius is 6.2 cm. The force is RCF = 1.118 × 10−5 × r (cm) × rpm2, in times the force of gravity (× g).
  5. Open the lid and take the tubes out
  6. Pour the cleared lysate off the pellet
  7. Keep samples: cleared lysate and pellet The pellet is taken up in lysis buffer to the lysate's volume before its sample is taken, so its lane can be compared.

C. The nickel column

  1. Swirl the resin, then 1 mL of slurry into the closed column The Ni-NTA beads settle in their bottle. 1 mL of 50% slurry gives a 0.5 mL bed, which holds about 4 mg of His-tagged protein.
  2. Equilibrate: 2.5 mL of lysis buffer, to the waste Five column volumes rinse the resin into the same buffer as the lysate. Never let the liquid fall below the top of the bed.
  3. Load the cleared lysate, collecting the flow-through Watch the top of the bed: the His-tagged protein binds to the nickel there.
  4. Wash twice with 4 mL of wash buffer 20 mM imidazole washes off bacterial proteins that stuck weakly; your protein stays bound.
  5. Elute: four fractions of 0.5 mL of elution buffer 250 mM imidazole competes the protein off the nickel. Set the micropipette to 500 µL and move the next tube under for each fraction.

D. Your samples

  1. Pool the fractions you chose The elution fractions ticked "Pool" in Table 1 of Part 1 go together into one tube: your purified protein. (To change your choice, go back to Part 1 and tick others.)
  2. Dilute the samples that need it The Bradford assay is close to a straight line only up to about 1 mg/mL, and the dye tolerates no more than 0.2 M imidazole. A dilution is made in dilution buffer, 200 µL in all. Write each dilution down: you multiply by it later.

E. How much protein: the Bradford assay

  1. 1 mL of dye into each cuvette (13 of them) One for the blank, one for each of the seven BSA standards, one for each sample. The dispenser on the dye bottle gives 1 mL a press.
  2. 20 µL of its sample into each cuvette Set the micropipette to 20 µL. The blank gets dilution buffer, S1 to S7 the BSA standards, each sample's cuvette its sample (its dilution, if you made one).
  3. Wait at least 5 minutes (read within the hour) The dye turns from brown to blue as it binds protein, and the colour takes a few minutes to develop. "Speed up the wait" runs the bench clock faster.
  4. Zero the spectrophotometer on the blank The blank is dye with no protein: zeroing on it means every reading after it is the colour the protein made.
  5. Read each cuvette at 595 nm, and record it A cuvette put in is read at once: its absorbance is on the screen. Record it in Table 3 or 4.

F. How brightly it glows: the fluorimeter

  1. Quartz cuvette: 1 mL of buffer, then 20 µL of a sample The fluorimeter's beam crosses the cuvette about 1 cm up, so the buffer goes in first. Rinse the cuvette between samples.
  2. Into the fluorimeter
  3. Find the peaks: scan the pooled fractions First an emission scan, exciting at 370 nm (these proteins all take up a little light in the near ultraviolet). Then set the emission to the peak you found and scan the excitation. Last, excite at the excitation peak and scan the emission again. A scan that reaches the top of the detector is too concentrated: dilute more.
  4. Read every sample at the two peaks, and record it Set the fluorimeter to your excitation and emission peaks, and read each sample at that same setting (Table 5). A reading of OVER means: dilute more.

G. Cast the gel

  1. Mix the resolving gel (10%, 10 mL) Acrylamide is a nerve poison until it has set: gloves on. The resolving gel uses the pH 8.8 Tris. APS and TEMED go in last: together they start the gel setting, so pour within a few minutes.
  2. Pour it between the plates, and cover it with water A thin layer of water on top keeps air off the gel (oxygen stops it setting) and makes its top edge flat.
  3. Let it set (about 45 minutes)
  4. Mix the stacking gel (4%, 10 mL) The stacking gel uses the pH 6.8 Tris and less acrylamide. APS and TEMED last again.
  5. Pour off the water, pour the stacking gel, put the comb in The comb's teeth leave the wells the samples go into.
  6. Let it set (about 30 minutes), then take the comb out
  7. The gel into the tank, and running buffer in

H. The samples

  1. 2x Laemmli buffer into every sample (1:1) SDS coats every protein with negative charge, the glycerol makes the sample sink into its well, and the blue dye shows where the run has got to.
  2. Heat at 95 °C for 5 minutes Heat with SDS unfolds every protein, so each runs by its size alone.
  3. Load 10 µL into each well (the ladder in one) Set the micropipette to 10 µL. Ten wells, eleven samples: choose what goes where, and write the plan down (Table 6).

I. Run the gel

  1. Lid on, leads in, 200 V until the blue dye front nears the bottom (about 40 minutes) The proteins carry negative charge, so they run toward the positive electrode (red) at the bottom.

J. Stain it and take its picture

  1. The gel into the tray: Coomassie for an hour, then destain for two Coomassie dyes every protein blue, and the gel too; destaining takes the blue back out of the gel and leaves the bands.
  2. The gel imager: take its picture White light for the stained gel. The blue light shows only what still glows.

5How well did it work? The purification table

The standard summary of a protein purification. For each sample: the total protein in it (its concentration from Table 4 times its volume), its total fluorescence (fluorescence per mL from Table 5 times its volume), the fluorescence per mg of protein (how pure it is), the yield (how much of the cleared lysate's fluorescence is in it) and the fold purification (how many times purer it is than the cleared lysate). The cleared lysate is the starting point: its yield is 100% and its purification 1.

  • The mass of a tagged protein is the protein's mass plus the tag's. tdTomato is two copies of one protein joined end to end, so it is about twice as heavy as the others.
  • SUMO runs heavier on an SDS gel than its mass: a His-SUMO protein's band appears about 5 to 6 kDa above the protein's mass plus 13.4. (Some proteins do not run exactly by their mass; SUMO is a well-known one.)
  • A gel cannot tell masses apart that are within a few kDa of each other: the spectra decide between proteins of the same size.
  • Two of the bacteria's own proteins stick to nickel and come off with the protein as faint bands: SlyD (it runs at about 27 kDa) and ArnA (74 kDa).
  • Some proteins have peaks close together. Use both peaks, and read them as closely as your scans allow.
