1What you will do

You are given a pellet of E. coli bacteria that have been made to produce a fluorescent protein: a protein that glows when light of the right colour shines on it. Each student's bacteria make their own unknown: one fluorescent protein, carrying one tag. Your job is to get that protein out pure and find out what it is.

  • Part 1: break the cells open, spin out the debris, catch your protein on a nickel column, wash away everything else, and release it into a few small tubes.
  • Part 2: measure how much protein each sample holds (Bradford assay), how much it glows (fluorescence), record its excitation and emission spectra, run a gel to see its size and purity, and sum it all up in a purification table.

The spectra tell you which fluorescent protein you have; the gel tells you which tag it carries. A reference sheet in Part 2 lists the possible proteins and tags. Making the match is your work, and your instructor marks it.

Every example on this page is made up. It uses an invented fluorescent protein, Lanternin, and an invented helper tag, Solubin. Neither is in the lab, so nothing here tells you what your unknown is.

2The His-tag and the nickel column

A bacterium holds thousands of different proteins. To pull out just one, it is given a handle: a short string of six histidines joined to one end of the protein. This is a His-tag. It is tiny: six histidines weigh about 0.8 kDa, against tens of kDa for the protein itself.

Why histidine holds on to nickel

Histidine's side chain is an imidazole ring, and one of its nitrogens can donate a lone pair to a metal ion, just as a ligand does in a metal complex. A nickel ion (Ni²⁺) has room for six such bonds.

The column is packed with tiny agarose beads, each covered with NTA (nitrilotriacetic acid) holding a nickel ion. NTA takes four of nickel's six places, which keeps the nickel firmly on the bead, and leaves two places free. Two histidines of the tag fill them. A single histidine on the surface of some other protein binds only weakly; six in a row, side by side, bind very strongly. This is Ni-NTA resin.

Why imidazole lets go of it

Free imidazole is the same ring as histidine's side chain, so it can sit in the same places on the nickel. It competes with the tag. How much imidazole is in a buffer decides what stays on the beads:

BufferImidazoleWhat it does
Lysis buffer10 mMEnough to keep bacterial proteins with a stray histidine or two from settling on the nickel, too little to move the six-histidine tag. The cells are broken in it, and the column is first rinsed with it.
Wash buffer20 mMA little stronger: rinses off more of the weakly held bacterial proteins while the tagged protein stays put.
Elution buffer250 mMSo much imidazole that it takes over the nickel's free places, and the tagged protein comes off.

All three buffers also hold 50 mM sodium phosphate at pH 8.0 and 300 mM NaCl. The salt stops proteins sticking to the beads just by their charge. The pH matters too: histidine's ring has a pKa of about 6, so at pH 8 it is not protonated and its nitrogen is free to bind nickel. (Lowering the pH to about 5 would also release the protein; this lab uses imidazole.)

For an organic chemist: it is a column whose stationary phase binds one group specifically, eluted by a change of solvent that competes for the same site. It separates by one particular bond, not by polarity, which is why one column can give nearly pure protein.

Sources: QIAexpressionist

A classmate makes the wash buffer with 250 mM imidazole by mistake. What happens to their protein during the wash?

It comes off the column and is lost in the wash tube. 250 mM imidazole out-competes the tag for the nickel, which is exactly what the elution buffer is meant to do later.

3Fusion tags

A fusion tag is a whole protein joined on, end to end, to the protein you want. The DNA for both is put together in one gene, so the bacterium makes one long chain: tag first, then the protein. The His-tag is usually put at the very front, so the fusion can still be caught on nickel.

Two fusion partners are used in this lab, as they are in many research labs:

  • SUMO (small ubiquitin-like modifier), a small protein from yeast;
  • MBP (maltose-binding protein), a much bigger protein from E. coli itself.

Why bother? A bacterium making large amounts of a foreign protein often fails to fold some of it. Misfolded chains stick together into clumps that do not dissolve; when the broken cells are spun, those clumps go to the bottom with the debris, in the pellet, and are lost. SUMO and MBP fold quickly and are very soluble, and joined to the front of another protein they help it fold and stay dissolved. So more of it ends up in the clear liquid that goes onto the column. In research, the tag is often cut off afterwards with a protease; in this lab it stays on.

Every tag adds its mass. A gel sorts proteins by size (section 9), so the same fluorescent protein runs at a different place with a different tag. Add the masses of the parts:

Two students make the same fluorescent protein, one with a His-tag only and one as a His-MBP fusion. Whose cells are likely to lose more of it to the pellet, and whose band runs higher on the gel?

The His-tag-only protein is more likely to end up partly in the pellet, because nothing helps it fold and stay dissolved. The MBP fusion runs higher on the gel (it moves less far), because MBP adds a lot of mass.

4Breaking the cells

The protein is inside the bacteria, so they must be broken open (lysed). The pellet is first stirred up evenly in cold lysis buffer (resuspended). Then two things break it:

  • Lysozyme, an enzyme (the one in egg white and tears), cuts the sugar chains of the bacterial cell wall, the rigid mesh that keeps a bacterium in shape. The protocol uses 1 mg/mL for 30 minutes on ice. Weakened cells break far more easily.
  • The sonicator, a metal probe dipped into the tube, vibrates about 20,000 times a second. It makes tiny bubbles in the liquid that collapse violently and tear the cells apart. The protocol is six bursts of 10 seconds, with 10 seconds of rest between them.

Why on ice, and why the rests

Almost all the sonicator's energy ends up as heat. Heat makes a protein unfold: its chain loses the shape it needs to work. Most fluorescent proteins are shaped like a can, with the part that glows (the chromophore) held tightly inside. Once the can unfolds, the chromophore is no longer held and the protein stops glowing. So the tube sits in ice, and each 10 second burst is followed by 10 seconds for the sample to cool. Two bursts back to back warm the sample much more.

After lysis the liquid holds everything that was in the cells: your protein, thousands of bacterial proteins, DNA, and the broken walls and membranes. This mixture is the lysate.

Sources: QIAexpressionist (lysis protocol)

In a real lab a sonicator gives off a piercing, high-pitched noise: wear ear protection, and keep fingers away from the probe while it runs.

Why is lysozyme added before the sonicator, rather than sonicating for longer?

Lysozyme weakens the cell walls, so fewer bursts break the cells. Fewer bursts means less heat, and less of the protein unfolds and stops glowing.

5Clearing the lysate

The lysate is cloudy with broken walls, membranes and clumps of unfolded protein. Poured straight onto the column, all that would clog the beads, and the column would run very slowly or stop. So the lysate is spun in a centrifuge at about 10,000 × g for 20 to 30 minutes, kept at 4 °C. The solids pack into a pellet at the bottom; the clear liquid above, the cleared lysate, is poured off and goes onto the column.

Balancing the rotor

A rotor spins thousands of times a minute. If one side is heavier, it wobbles hard: at best the centrifuge stops, at worst the rotor is damaged. So tubes always go in opposite places, holding equal volumes (equal masses). With one sample, make a balance tube of water filled to the same level, and put it straight across from the sample.

rpm or × g?

What separates the solids is the force, given as relative centrifugal force (RCF): how many times stronger than gravity, written "× g". The same speed in rpm (revolutions per minute) gives a stronger force in a bigger rotor, so protocols give × g, and you work out the speed for your own rotor:

RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × rpm²

r is the distance from the centre of the rotor to the sample, in centimetres.

A made-up rotor with r = 8.0 cm, spun at 9,000 rpm:
RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 8.0 × 9,000² = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 8.0 × 81,000,000 ≈ 7,200 × g
To reach 10,000 × g in the same rotor, turn the formula round:
rpm = √(10,000 ÷ (1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 8.0)) ≈ 10,600 rpm

Notice that rpm is squared: doubling the speed gives four times the force. In the lab, use your own centrifuge's radius, which the lab page gives.

Try it: rpm and × g

In a real lab never start an unbalanced centrifuge, never open the lid while the rotor turns, and wait for it to stop completely.

A rotor has r = 10 cm and is spun at 6,000 rpm. About what force is that?

1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × 6,000² = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × 36,000,000 ≈ 4,000 × g. Not enough for a protocol that asks for 10,000 × g.

6The column, step by step

Ni-NTA resin is sold as a 50% slurry: half beads, half liquid. One millilitre of slurry poured into a small column settles into a bed of 0.5 mL of beads. Liquid then runs through the bed by gravity, out of the tip, into whatever tube is under it. A tube is put under the tip for each step, so every drop is kept and can be tested later.

  1. Equilibrate

    Run lysis buffer through the bed (about five times its volume). This fills the bed with the same buffer, at the same pH and imidazole, as the sample about to go on.

    into the waste beaker

  2. Load

    Pour on the cleared lysate. As it runs through, the tagged protein catches on the first beads it meets, at the top of the bed. A coloured protein shows there as a band. Everything that does not bind runs straight through.

    collect: flow-through

  3. Wash

    Run wash buffer (20 mM imidazole) through, twice. Bacterial proteins that stuck weakly are rinsed out; the tagged protein stays on the nickel.

    collect: wash 1, wash 2

  4. Elute

    Add elution buffer (250 mM imidazole) in small portions, each into its own tube. The imidazole takes the protein's places on the nickel, and the band slides down the bed and out of the tip.

    collect: elution fractions E1, E2, E3, E4

Never let the bed run dry

Always add the next liquid before the last has sunk into the beads. If the liquid drains below the top of the bed, air gets in, the beads dry and crack, and open channels form. The next liquid then rushes down the channels instead of flowing past every bead, so protein runs through without binding, and washing and eluting work badly. A column that ran dry has to be stirred up and packed again.

Keep every tube until the end. Which tubes hold your protein is something you find out at the bench, with a lamp (section 7) and later with the measurements of Part 2. If something went wrong, the tubes show where the protein went.

Sources: QIAexpressionist (packed column protocol)

Why is the column rinsed with lysis buffer before the lysate goes on, and not with water?

So the bed is already at the lysate's pH, salt and 10 mM imidazole. Then the protein binds under the same conditions from the very first drop, and weakly binding bacterial proteins are kept off from the start.

7Finding the protein: fluorescence

A fluorescent molecule absorbs light of one colour and, a few billionths of a second later, gives off light of another colour. The light given off always has a longer wavelength (lower energy) than the light absorbed, because the molecule loses a little energy as heat in between. Blue light in can come out green; green light in can come out red; never the other way round.

Two spectra

  • The excitation spectrum: the detector is set to one wavelength of emitted light, and the wavelength of the light shining in is scanned. It shows which colours make the protein glow, and how well.
  • The emission spectrum: the light shining in is set to one wavelength, and the detector is scanned. It shows which colours the protein gives off.

Each spectrum has a peak, its maximum. The pair of maxima, excitation and emission, is like a fingerprint: comparing both with a table of known proteins is how a fluorescent protein is identified. To find a peak accurately, scan across it in small steps.

One colour in room light, another under the lamp

In ordinary white light, a solution of a fluorescent protein looks the colour that is left over after it absorbs its own band of colours. Under a lamp in the dark, it glows the colour it emits. These are usually different colours, so do not be surprised when a tube looks one colour on the bench and glows another.

Lanternin absorbs orange-red light (around 655 nm). White light with its orange-red taken out looks blue-green, so a tube of Lanternin looks blue-green on the bench. Under a lamp whose light it absorbs, in the dark, it glows deep red (around 680 nm). A lamp of a colour the protein does not absorb makes it glow weakly or not at all.

In a real lab an ultraviolet lamp burns eyes and skin like strong sun: wear UV-blocking glasses or a face shield, keep skin covered, and never look into the lamp.

A made-up protein has its excitation maximum at 412 nm. Could its emission maximum be at 400 nm?

No. Emitted light always has a longer wavelength than the light absorbed, so the emission maximum must be above 412 nm.

8How much protein: the Bradford assay

The Bradford reagent holds a dye, Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250, in acid. On its own the dye is reddish brown; when it binds protein it turns blue, and its absorbance peak moves from 465 to 595 nm. The more protein, the more blue. In the lab, 20 µL of sample is mixed with 1 mL of dye, left at least 5 minutes (and no longer than an hour), and read at 595 nm.

The standard curve

The dye gives no number by itself, so it is calibrated with standards: solutions of a protein of known concentration, here BSA (bovine serum albumin), from 0.125 to 1 mg/mL. Plot their absorbances against concentration to get a line; then any sample's absorbance can be turned into a concentration, as in the enzyme lab. The blank (zero) is the same buffer with no protein, so the buffer's own colour is not counted.

Diluting, and the dilution factor

Two reasons to dilute a sample before reading it:

  • It reads above the top standard. Beyond the standards the line is no longer known (it bends), so the number would be wrong.
  • Imidazole upsets the dye above 0.2 M (200 mM). The elution fractions hold 250 mM, so they must be diluted before they are read. A 1 in 5 dilution brings them to 50 mM. (The blank should be elution buffer diluted the same way.)

The curve gives the concentration in the diluted sample. Multiply by the dilution factor to get the concentration in the tube you started with:

dilution factor = total volume ÷ volume of sample

Made up: 10 µL of an elution fraction plus 40 µL of water makes 50 µL, a dilution factor of 50 ÷ 10 = 5. The diluted sample reads 0.51; on a curve where 1 mg/mL reads 0.85, that is
0.51 ÷ 0.85 = 0.60 mg/mL in the diluted sample, and 0.60 × 5 = 3.0 mg/mL in the fraction

Try it: from absorbance to the protein in your tube

Uses the made-up curve above. The sample is an elution fraction (250 mM imidazole) diluted with water.

Sources: Bio-Rad Quick Start Bradford manual

A fraction diluted 1 in 10 reads 0.34 on the made-up curve (1 mg/mL reads 0.85). What is the protein concentration in the fraction?

0.34 ÷ 0.85 = 0.40 mg/mL in the diluted sample; × 10 = 4.0 mg/mL in the fraction. Forgetting to multiply by the dilution factor is the commonest slip.

9How big, and how pure: SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) sorts proteins by size. Each protein in a sample becomes a band; one band in a lane means a pure protein, many bands mean a mixture.

Preparing samples

Each sample is mixed 1 to 1 with Laemmli sample buffer and heated at 95 °C for 5 minutes. In the buffer:

  • SDS, a detergent with a negative charge. With the heat, it unfolds every protein into a floppy chain and coats it with negative charge, about the same amount per length for every protein. Shape and the protein's own charge no longer matter: only size does.
  • 2-Mercaptoethanol, which breaks disulfide (S–S) bridges, so the chain is fully undone.
  • Glycerol, which makes the sample dense, so it sinks into the well.
  • Bromophenol blue, a small blue dye that runs ahead of the proteins and shows how far the run has gone.

The gel and the run

The gel is a mesh of polyacrylamide, cast between two glass plates. A stacking gel on top (4%, pH 6.8) squeezes each sample into a thin line; the resolving gel below (10% here) does the sorting. Small proteins slip through the mesh quickly and run far; big ones are held back and stay near the top. With 200 V applied, the negatively coated proteins move toward the positive electrode, the red lead: "run to red". Afterwards the gel is stained with Coomassie, so every protein band shows blue.

Reading a mass from the ladder

One lane holds a ladder: proteins of known mass. Measure how far each ladder band ran from the top of the resolving gel. Plotted against distance, the log of the mass falls on a nearly straight line. Measure your band's distance, go up to the line, and read across.

Made-up ladder band (kDa)1208560453525

The sample band ran 22 mm, between the 85 kDa band (21 mm) and the 60 kDa band (30 mm). Work in logs: log 85 = 1.929 and log 60 = 1.778. The band is 1 mm of the 9 mm between them past the 85 kDa band, so
log mass = 1.929 − (1 ÷ 9) × (1.929 − 1.778) = 1.929 − 0.017 = 1.912, and mass = 101.912 ≈ 82 kDa
The straight line through all six ladder bands gives about 83 kDa. Either way, it fits His-Solubin-Lanternin (81 kDa) and not His-Lanternin (47 kDa). A gel reads a mass to within a few percent, no better.

Look at the other lanes too: the lysate lane shows many bands (all the bacterial proteins), and a clean elution lane shows few. That is how a gel shows purity.

Sources: Mini-PROTEAN Tetra manual, Bio-Rad Electrophoresis Guide, Laemmli sample buffer

In a real lab acrylamide is a nerve poison until it has set into a gel: wear gloves and goggles while casting. The gel tank carries a high voltage: put the lid on before the power supply is switched on.

On the made-up gel above, a band runs 36 mm. About what mass is it?

Between 60 kDa (30 mm) and 45 kDa (37 mm): log mass = 1.778 − (6 ÷ 7) × (1.778 − 1.653) = 1.671, so about 47 kDa, the mass of His-Lanternin.

10The purification table

A purification table sums up a protein prep: at each stage, how much protein there is in all, how much of your protein, and how pure it is. Total protein comes from the Bradford assay. Your protein is followed by its glow: fluorescence counts only the fluorescent protein, the way an enzyme's activity counts only that enzyme.

ColumnHow it is worked outWhat it tells you
Total protein (mg)concentration (mg/mL) × volume (mL)all the protein at that stage
Total fluorescencefluorescence per mL × volume (mL)how much of your protein is there
Specific fluorescencetotal fluorescence ÷ total protein (mg)how pure it is: the more of the protein is yours, the higher
Yield (%)total fluorescence ÷ total fluorescence of the cleared lysate × 100how much of your protein you kept
Fold purificationspecific fluorescence ÷ specific fluorescence of the cleared lysatehow many times purer it became

The cleared lysate is the starting point, so its yield is 100% and its fold purification is 1. A worked example, with made-up numbers (fluorescence in the fluorimeter's own units):

StageVolume (mL)Protein (mg/mL)Total protein (mg)Fluorescence per mLTotal fluorescenceSpecific fluorescence (per mg)YieldFold
Cleared lysate10.06.0601,50015,000250100%1
Pooled fractions2.02.55.06,00012,0002,40080%9.6

Pooled fractions: total protein 2.5 × 2.0 = 5.0 mg; total fluorescence 6,000 × 2.0 = 12,000; specific fluorescence 12,000 ÷ 5.0 = 2,400 per mg; yield 12,000 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = 80%; fold purification 2,400 ÷ 250 = 9.6.

Yield and fold purification pull against each other: keeping only the cleanest fractions raises the purity and lowers the yield. In the lab the table also has rows for the flow-through and the washes, which account for the rest of the protein.

A made-up cleared lysate has 50 mg of protein and a total fluorescence of 20,000. The pooled fractions have 2.0 mg and 16,000. Work out the yield and the fold purification.

Yield: 16,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 80%. Specific fluorescence: lysate 20,000 ÷ 50 = 400 per mg; pooled 16,000 ÷ 2.0 = 8,000 per mg. Fold purification: 8,000 ÷ 400 = 20.
