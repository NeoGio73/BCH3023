Part A

Mirror images, measured

Two molecules can have the same atoms, joined the same way, and still differ as your two hands do. This part is about telling them apart and measuring how much of each a sample holds.

1Enantiomers and enantiomeric excess

A carbon with four different groups on it is a stereocenter. Its two mirror-image arrangements cannot be laid on top of each other, any more than a left hand fits a right glove.

Hold your hands up, palms facing you. They are mirror images, and no amount of turning makes one lie exactly on the other: the thumbs end up on opposite sides. Molecules can be like that. A carbon atom with four different groups attached can be built in two ways that are mirror images of each other, and the two are called enantiomers. A molecule that has such a carbon (a stereocenter) is chiral, from the Greek for hand.

In most ways the two enantiomers are the same compound. They have the same boiling point, the same solubility, the same TLC spot, the same NMR spectrum in an ordinary solvent, because all of those depend on bonds and distances, which are identical in a mirror image. They differ only when they meet something that is itself handed: polarized light (topic 2), a chiral column (topic 3), or an enzyme (Part B), whose pocket is built from L-amino acids and is as handed as your glove. That is why the two enantiomers of a drug can act differently in the body, and why carvone smells of caraway as one enantiomer and of spearmint as the other.

Naming them: R and S

Each stereocenter is named with the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) rules, in three steps.

  1. Rank the four groups by the atomic number of the atom attached to the stereocenter: the higher the number, the higher the rank. For the alcohols in this lab that puts OH first and H last. If two groups start with the same atom (two carbons, say), move out one bond and compare what those carbons carry; a carbon bearing O beats one bearing only C and H, and a carbon with more carbons on it beats one with fewer. A double bond counts as that atom twice.
  2. Turn the molecule so the lowest-ranked group (usually H) points away from you.
  3. Follow 1 → 2 → 3. Clockwise is R (Latin rectus, right); anticlockwise is S (sinister, left).

Keep one thing in mind: the letter comes from the ranks, not from the shape. Change one group for another that ranks differently, and the same arrangement in space can change its letter. That will matter in topic 7.

How much of each: the enantiomeric excess

A sample with equal amounts of the two enantiomers is racemic, and most ordinary syntheses give exactly that, because nothing in a flask of achiral reagents prefers one hand. How far a sample is from racemic is its enantiomeric excess:

ee = (major − minor) / (major + minor) × 100%An 80 : 20 mixture has ee = (80 − 20) / 100 = 60%. A pure enantiomer has 100% ee; a racemate 0%.

The same information is sometimes written as an enantiomeric ratio, er 80 : 20. To go back from an ee to the ratio: the major share is (100 + ee) / 2, so 60% ee means 80 : 20. An ee is only as good as the method that measured it, which is what the next two topics are about.

From a ratio to ee, and back

  • A carbon with four different groups has two mirror-image forms, the enantiomers.
  • They differ only toward handed things: polarized light, a chiral column, an enzyme.
  • R and S come from the CIP ranks (H away, follow 1 → 2 → 3), so they are a label, not a shape.
  • ee = (major − minor) / (major + minor).

Check yourself

  1. A sample is 92 : 8. What is its ee?
    Answer

    (92 − 8) / 100 = 84% ee.

  2. A sample has 50% ee. What share of it is the minor enantiomer?
    Answer

    25%: the major share is (100 + 50) / 2 = 75, so the mixture is 75 : 25.

  3. Why can an ordinary NMR spectrum not tell you the ee?
    Answer

    In an achiral solvent the two enantiomers have identical spectra: every distance and every bond is the same in a mirror image.

  4. In the figure, which group of lactic acid is ranked 2, and why does it beat CH3?
    Answer

    COOH. Both start with a carbon, but the COOH carbon carries (O, O, O) and the CH3 carbon (H, H, H); oxygen outranks hydrogen.

2Optical rotation and the polarimeter

A solution of one enantiomer turns the plane of polarized light; a solution of its mirror image turns it by the same angle the other way. That turn is the oldest way of telling enantiomers apart, and it is how Session 1’s ee is measured.

Ordinary light vibrates in every direction across its path. A polarizer lets through only the light vibrating in one plane. When that plane-polarized light passes through a solution of a chiral compound, the plane comes out turned by an angle, α. The two enantiomers turn it by the same amount in opposite directions, so a racemic solution turns it not at all.

A compound that turns the plane clockwise, as seen by someone looking toward the lamp, is dextrorotatory and written (+); anticlockwise is levorotatory, (−). These signs are measured, and they have nothing to do with R and S, which are worked out from the drawing. An (R) compound can be (+) or (−); only an experiment tells you which.

The angle grows with how many molecules the light passes: a longer tube or a stronger solution gives a bigger turn. Dividing by both gives a property of the compound, the specific rotation:

[α] = α / (l · c)α in degrees; l in decimetres (the usual tube is 100 mm, which is 1 dm); c in grams per millilitre. Tables write c in g per 100 mL, so “c 1.0” means 1 g in 100 mL, which is 0.010 g/mL. The solvent, the temperature and the wavelength are given too (the D in [α]D is the sodium D line), because [α] changes with all three.

If the pure enantiomer’s [α] is known, the ratio of yours to it is the optical purity, and it is taken as the ee:

ee ≈ [α]sample / [α]pure × 100%Same solvent, same concentration range, same temperature, or the comparison is not fair.

What goes wrong, and why

  • No blank. The instrument’s zero is set with the tube full of the pure solvent, because the tube’s glass and the solvent itself can turn the plane a little. Skip it and every reading carries that offset.
  • An air bubble in the light path scatters and bends the light, and the reading is wrong. Fill the tube slowly, tilted, until it is brimming, and look along it before you cap it.
  • Something else in the sample. Suppose 10% of the mass you weighed is a leftover starting material that does not rotate light. Then c, as you calculated it, is 10% too high for the compound that does rotate, and [α] comes out 10% low, and so does the ee. If you know the compound’s share of the mass (an NMR tells you, topic 12), divide the ee by that share.
  • Small angles. At c 1.0 in a 1 dm tube, a compound with [α] = +40° turns the light by only 40 × 1 × 0.010 = +0.40°. A digital instrument reads that to a few thousandths of a degree; an eye on a manual scale to about a twentieth, which is already 12% of the reading.

A made-up polarimeter reading

Compound Q (made up): its pure (+) enantiomer has [α] = +60.0° (c 1.0, chloroform, 25 °C). Enter your own reading and amounts.

Worked example (made up)

248 mg of Q made up to 25.0 mL is 0.248 g / 25.0 mL = 0.00992 g per mL. In a 1 dm tube it reads α = +0.512°. [α] = 0.512 / (1 × 0.00992) = +51.6°, so ee ≈ 51.6 / 60.0 = 86%. If an NMR showed the sample was 95% Q by mass, the ee of the Q itself is 86 / 0.95 = 91%.

  • Polarized light comes out of a chiral solution with its plane turned by α; the mirror image turns it the other way.
  • [α] = α / (l c), l in dm, c in g/mL; ee ≈ [α] / [α]pure.
  • (+) and (−) are measured; R and S are worked out. They are not the same thing.
  • Zero on a blank; no bubbles; correct for anything else in the weighed sample.

Check yourself

  1. c is written “c 2.0”. What is it in g per mL?
    Answer

    2.0 g per 100 mL = 0.020 g per mL.

  2. You forget the blank and the instrument reads +0.05° with only solvent in it. What happens to a sample reading of +0.40°?
    Answer

    It reads about +0.45°, 12% too high, and so does the ee worked from it.

  3. Why would a manual polarimeter be a poor choice for a sample that turns only +0.40°?
    Answer

    Reading a scale by eye to about ±0.05° puts the ee out by over 10%.

  4. A compound is (S) and (−). Is its (R) enantiomer (+) or (−)?
    Answer

    (+): the mirror image turns the plane by the same angle the other way. But you could not have predicted the (S) one was (−) from its drawing; that had to be measured.

3Chiral gas chromatography

A gas chromatograph separates the compounds in a mixture by how long each one is held back on its way through a long, thin tube. A chiral coating in that tube holds one enantiomer a little longer than the other, so they come out as two peaks, and the ee is read from their areas.

What a GC does

A drop of the sample is injected into a hot port, where it boils. A stream of carrier gas (helium or hydrogen) sweeps the vapour into the column: a hollow silica tube thinner than a hair and 25 or 30 metres long, coiled in an oven, its inside wall coated with a thin film. Each compound keeps dissolving into the film and evaporating back into the gas; the longer it stays dissolved, the later it reaches the far end. There a flame ionization detector burns it and gives a signal proportional to how much carbon passes. The result is a chromatogram: a peak for each compound at its retention time, and an integrator that reports each peak’s time and area.

Why a chiral column separates enantiomers

On an ordinary column the two enantiomers come out together, as one peak: the film cannot tell them apart. A chiral column’s film contains a chiral molecule, most often a cyclodextrin: a ring of six, seven or eight glucose units with a cone-shaped hollow in the middle, its hydroxyl groups capped with methyl or acetyl groups so that it dissolves in the film. Glucose is itself chiral, so the hollow is handed. One enantiomer fits into it a little better and keeps slipping in and out as it is carried along; its mirror image fits poorly. The one that fits spends more of its time held, and reaches the detector later. The difference is small, often seconds, but a long column makes it enough.

The two enantiomers burn alike, so their areas are in the ratio of their amounts, and the ee comes straight from them:

ee = |A1 − A2| / (A1 + A2) × 100%A1 and A2 are the two peaks’ areas. The racemate gives two equal peaks, ee = 0.

Which peak is which?

Nothing in the chromatogram says which peak is R. The order depends on the compound and on the coating: the same pair can come off R first on one cyclodextrin and S first on another. The only sure way is a standard: a pure (or nearly pure) sample of one enantiomer, bought or made, and run the same way. Its peak’s time names that peak in your sample. In the lab you are given the pure (R) alcohol for this.

What goes wrong, and why

  • Too much. The film can only dissolve so much. An undiluted sample overloads it: the peaks broaden and lean, and two peaks close together merge into one, which the integrator then reports as a single peak. Dilute until the peaks are sharp.
  • Solid in the vial. Anything that keeps reacting (a catalyst, a bead of enzyme) must be filtered out when the sample is taken, or the vial goes on changing after you sampled it, and the chromatogram is not of the moment you meant.
  • Peaks that are not yours. The solvent, a reagent in excess and small by-products all give peaks. Run the starting materials on their own first so you know where they come off.

A made-up chromatogram and its standard

Compound W (made up) on a made-up chiral column. Point at a peak to see the integrator’s numbers; switch between the sample and the pure (S)-W standard.

Point at a peak.

  • A GC holds each compound back by how long it dissolves in the column’s film; the integrator gives each peak’s time and area.
  • A chiral film (a cyclodextrin) is handed: one enantiomer fits it better and comes off later. Two peaks; ee from their areas.
  • Name the peaks from a standard run the same way. Never from a remembered order.
  • Dilute the sample, and filter out anything that keeps reacting.

Check yourself

  1. Peaks at 6.10 and 6.42 min have areas 12 450 and 2 050. What is the ee?
    Answer

    (12 450 − 2 050) / 14 500 = 71.7%.

  2. In the made-up chromatogram above, which enantiomer is the major one?
    Answer

    Run the standard: (S)-W comes off with the first peak, and the first peak is the small one. The major enantiomer is (R)-W.

  3. An undiluted sample shows one broad peak where a diluted one shows two. What ee would you get from the undiluted one, and why is it wrong?
    Answer

    100%, since there is only one peak to measure; the two enantiomers have run together, not become one.

  4. Why does an ordinary (achiral) column give one peak for a racemate?
    Answer

    Its film is not handed, so it dissolves both enantiomers for exactly the same time, and they arrive together.

Part B

Enzymes as reagents

An enzyme’s pocket is chiral, so it treats mirror-image molecules differently. Session 1 uses that to make one enantiomer out of a flat ketone; Session 2 uses it to sort the two enantiomers of an alcohol.

4Baker’s yeast reduces a ketone

Living yeast cells carry reductases that hand a hydride from NADPH to a ketone’s carbonyl from one face only, so the alcohol that forms is mostly one enantiomer. You do not isolate the enzyme; you borrow the whole cell.

Baker’s yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, is used here as a bag of enzymes that looks after itself. Fed sugar, the cells make NADPH (mostly through the pentose phosphate pathway, which spends glucose to make it), and several NADPH-dependent ketoreductases in the cell use it to reduce a ketone that is added to the broth. The cells regenerate NADPH as long as they have sugar, which is why a fermentation is fed, and why the reduction takes days rather than minutes: the whole culture is doing a little at a time.

A ketone’s carbonyl carbon is flat, with its three neighbours in one plane, so hydride can arrive from either face. A chemical reducing agent such as sodium borohydride comes from both faces equally and gives a racemate. In a reductase’s pocket the ketone is held with one face against the protein, so hydride can only reach the other, and the new stereocenter forms with one configuration.

For most simple ketones, yeast’s main reductases deliver the hydride to the same face, which gives the alcohol in which, drawn as in the figure, the OH ends up on the hashed bond when the larger group is on the left: Prelog’s rule, which for simple ketones usually means the (S) alcohol. But a yeast cell has several reductases, and some prefer the other face, so a yeast product is rarely 100% one enantiomer. Which enzymes do most of the work depends on how the culture is run: how much ketone it meets at once (a little at a time favours the enzymes that bind it tightly), whether it is fed sugar or starved, and how it is aerated. That is why every student’s fermentation in Session 1 is run a little differently, and why each gets a different ee.

The bench, in outline

  1. Wake the yeast in warm water with sugar. Yeast likes about 30 to 40 °C; water near 55 °C or hotter kills it, and dead yeast reduces nothing. A live culture foams within minutes: fermentation makes carbon dioxide, and the bubbles are held by the yeast’s own proteins.
  2. Add the ketone, stir, and wait, open to the air (some oxygen keeps the cells healthier). Follow the reduction by TLC until the ketone’s spot is gone; stopping earlier leaves ketone in the product.
  3. Work up: filter off the cells through Celite (topic 8), salt the filtrate and extract the product (topic 9), dry and evaporate (topic 10), weigh, and measure the rotation (topic 2) and the NMR (topic 12).

  • Whole cells: the reductases inside, and the sugar that keeps their NADPH coming.
  • The flat carbonyl is held with one face covered, so hydride reaches only the other: a new stereocenter, mostly one enantiomer.
  • Several reductases compete, so the ee depends on how the fermentation is run.
  • Warm water wakes yeast; hot water kills it. Foam means it is alive.

Check yourself

  1. Why does a yeast reduction need sugar, though sugar is not reduced?
    Answer

    The cells burn it to regenerate the NADPH the reductases use up. Without sugar the NADPH runs out and the reduction stops.

  2. The water for the yeast was 70 °C. What will you see, and what will the ketone do?
    Answer

    No foam: the yeast is dead, and the ketone is not reduced.

  3. Why does sodium borohydride give a racemate where yeast does not?
    Answer

    Borohydride is small and achiral, and reaches both faces of the flat carbonyl equally. The reductase holds the ketone with one face against the protein, so only the other face is open to the hydride.

5A lipase on beads, in hexane

Lipases are made to hydrolyse fats in water. In a dry organic solvent the same machinery runs the other way and makes esters, and it is choosy about which alcohol it will esterify. Session 2 uses one that comes stuck to plastic beads.

What the enzyme is

Candida antarctica lipase B, CAL-B, is a small, sturdy enzyme from a yeast first found in Antarctica. Its active site holds a catalytic triad: a serine (Ser105), a histidine (His224) and an aspartate (Asp187). The aspartate steadies the histidine, the histidine takes the proton off the serine’s OH, and the serine’s oxygen, now a strong nucleophile, attacks the carbonyl carbon of an ester. The ester’s alkoxy group leaves, and the acyl group is left parked on the serine: the acyl-enzyme. In the second half of the cycle a nucleophile takes the acyl group off again. In water that nucleophile is water (hydrolysis: the lipase’s job in nature). In a dry solvent the only nucleophile about is an alcohol, so the acyl group goes onto the alcohol instead, and an ester is made.

What the beads are

Novozym 435 is CAL-B soaked into small beads of a porous acrylic plastic, about half a millimetre across. The beads are only the support: they do no chemistry. What they do is keep the enzyme in one place, so that it can be filtered off in a minute to stop the reaction, rinsed and used again, and so that a few milligrams can be weighed out and added like any other reagent. The choosing between enantiomers happens inside the enzyme’s pocket (topic 7), not on the plastic.

Why hexane, and why it must be dry

Enzymes are made for water, but many lipases work in dry organic solvents, because a thin layer of water stays bound to the protein and keeps it folded and flexible. Hexane dissolves the alcohol and the vinyl acetate, does not dissolve the enzyme, and does not compete with the alcohol. Any extra water does compete: it takes the acyl group off the serine before the alcohol can (making acetic acid, which is useless), and it lets the enzyme hydrolyse the ester already made back to the alcohol. So the hexane is kept over molecular sieves, and the beads are kept dry and cold.

Why vinyl acetate

Any ester could acylate the serine, but most would set the cycle running in both directions: with ethyl acetate, the ethanol set free would attack the acyl-enzyme too, and the reaction would stop at an equilibrium. Vinyl acetate frees vinyl alcohol, which at once turns into its tautomer, acetaldehyde, a molecule that cannot attack the acyl-enzyme. Nothing runs back; the acylation is one-way. It is used in excess (2.5 equivalents) so that it is never in short supply as the reaction goes on.

R–OH + CH2=CH–O–C(O)CH3 → R–O–C(O)CH3 + CH3CHOAn alcohol and vinyl acetate give the alcohol’s acetate and acetaldehyde, by way of the acyl-enzyme. The acetaldehyde is a small, volatile by-product; it shows as an early peak on the GC.

Why 40 °C, shaken

Warmer is faster, as for any reaction, but a lipase tells the two enantiomers apart less well as it gets warmer (topic 6 says how that is measured), and too hot unfolds it. About 40 °C is the usual compromise. Shaking keeps the beads moving through the solution so that fresh alcohol keeps reaching them.

  • Ser–His–Asp: the serine is acylated by the acyl donor, then hands the acyl group to the alcohol.
  • The beads are the enzyme’s support: easy to add, to filter off and to use again. The chemistry is in the enzyme.
  • Dry hexane, or water takes the acyl group and hydrolyses the ester.
  • Vinyl acetate frees vinyl alcohol, which becomes acetaldehyde: the acylation cannot run back.

Check yourself

  1. Why would ethyl acetate be a worse acyl donor than vinyl acetate?
    Answer

    It frees ethanol, which can attack the acyl-enzyme too: the reaction runs both ways and stops at an equilibrium.

  2. What does the histidine of the triad do?
    Answer

    It takes the serine’s proton (helped by the aspartate), making the serine oxygen a strong nucleophile.

  3. Your hexane came from the wash bottle on the bench rather than the dried bottle. Name two things that go wrong.
    Answer

    Water takes the acyl group off the serine, so less ester is made and acetic acid forms; and the ester already made is hydrolysed back to the alcohol, so the ee of the alcohol left falls.

  4. You filter the beads out of the vial. Does the reaction continue?
    Answer

    No: the enzyme is on the beads, and nothing in the solution can make the ester without it. Filtering off the beads is how the reaction is stopped.

6Kinetic resolution and E

If an enzyme acylates one enantiomer of a racemic alcohol much faster than the other, stopping halfway leaves one enantiomer as the alcohol and the other as the acetate. The two can then be separated, because they are now different compounds.

Start with a racemic alcohol: half (R), half (S). Put it with the lipase and vinyl acetate. The fast enantiomer is turned into its acetate; the slow one mostly stays as it was. Two things change as the reaction runs:

  • The alcohol left is more and more the slow enantiomer: its ee, ees (s for substrate), rises toward 100%.
  • The acetate made is mostly the fast enantiomer: its ee, eep (p for product), starts high and slowly falls, because once the fast enantiomer is used up, the enzyme has only the slow one left to work on.

Only half of the starting material can become the fast enantiomer’s acetate, so a resolution is stopped near 50% conversion (c). Stopping early leaves the alcohol only partly enriched; going far past 50% makes the alcohol purer still but the acetate less pure. The reaction is called a kinetic resolution because it rests on a difference in rate, not on a difference in stability: given enough time, the enzyme would acetylate everything.

E: how well the enzyme tells them apart

The enzyme’s enantioselectivity, E, is the ratio of the rates at which it handles the two enantiomers (strictly, the ratio of their kcat/KM). E = 1 means it cannot tell them apart; E = 100 means the fast one goes a hundred times faster. Because the two enantiomers compete for the same enzyme throughout, E ties the conversion and the two ee values together, whatever the time scale (Chen, Fujimoto, Girdaukas and Sih, 1982). You do not need to weigh anything to get c:

c = ees / (ees + eep)From the two ee values alone, as fractions.

E = ln[(1 − c)(1 − ees)] / ln[(1 − c)(1 + ees)]c and ees as fractions (0.40, not 40). Both logarithms are negative; the ratio is positive.

E above about 100 is excellent: both products come out nearly pure at 50%. Below about 15, neither does, however carefully you stop. Above about 200 the formula becomes very sensitive to the last digit of the ee values (an ee of 99.0% and one of 99.5% give very different E), so a large E is a range, not a number, and “>200” is how it is usually reported.

How E shapes a resolution

The ee of the alcohol left (blue) and of the acetate made (orange) as the conversion rises, for the E you choose. Try E = 5, 20 and 200.

Worked example (made up)

A resolution stopped and separated gives the alcohol at ees = 62% and the acetate at eep = 93%. c = 0.62 / (0.62 + 0.93) = 0.400, so 40%. E = ln[(0.600)(0.380)] / ln[(0.600)(1.620)] = ln 0.228 / ln 0.972 = (−1.478) / (−0.0284) = about 52.

  • Fast enantiomer → acetate; slow one stays as the alcohol. Stop near 50%.
  • ees rises as the reaction runs; eep falls after 50%.
  • c from the two ee values; E from c and ees.
  • A large E is a range: the formula swings with the last digit.

Check yourself

  1. ees = 30%, eep = 90%. What is c?
    Answer

    0.30 / 1.20 = 25%.

  2. Why does the acetate’s ee fall if you run far past 50%?
    Answer

    Once the fast enantiomer is used up, the slow one is acylated too, and its acetate dilutes the fast one’s.

  3. With E = 5, can you get both products pure by stopping at exactly 50%?
    Answer

    No. Use the plot: at E = 5 and 50%, both ee values are only about 50%.

  4. Why is the best possible yield of the acetate 50%?
    Answer

    Only half the starting material is the fast enantiomer; the other half is meant to stay as the alcohol.

7The Kazlauskas rule

For secondary alcohols, CAL-B and many other lipases prefer the enantiomer that fits their pocket with its larger group in the larger space. The rule lets you predict which enantiomer will react faster from a drawing, and the lab lets you test the prediction.

A secondary alcohol’s stereocenter carries an OH, an H, and two carbon groups, one larger (L) and one smaller, the medium group (M). In 1991 Kazlauskas and co-workers compared hundreds of lipase reactions and found a pattern: the enantiomer acylated faster is the one in which, with the H pointing away from you, going from OH to L to M runs clockwise.

CAL-B’s crystal structure shows why. Beside the serine that carries the acetyl group, the active site has a small, narrow slot (the stereospecificity pocket) and a roomy space open to the solvent. For the alcohol’s OH to reach the acetyl group, one of its two carbon groups must sit in the slot; only the medium one fits. The enantiomer that can put M in the slot while L points out into the open space reacts fast. Its mirror image, to bring its OH to the same place, would have to push L into the slot, and L is too big. It still reacts, through less comfortable arrangements, but slowly. The ratio of the two rates is E.

Which letter is that?

If L also outranks M by the CIP rules (topic 1), the faster enantiomer is the (R) one: OH is 1, L is 2, M is 3, and 1 → 2 → 3 clockwise is R. That is true for most of the alcohols in the lab. But a medium group that carries a heavier atom, or a double or triple bond, can outrank a longer alkyl chain even though it is smaller, and then the same shape gets the letter (S). Always work out the letter from the ranks; never assume it. Your report asks for the letter, and your instructor will look at how you got it.

When the rule works less well

The rule is about sizes. When L and M differ a lot (a phenyl and a methyl, say), the enzyme chooses strongly and E is large. When they are alike (an ethyl and a methyl), the enzyme can hardly tell the enantiomers apart and E is small. A group right next to the stereocenter that is bulky in the wrong direction can slow the whole reaction down. You will see some of this in the class’s results, since each student has a different alcohol.

Worked example: 1-cyclohexylethanol (not one of the lab’s alcohols)

L = cyclohexyl, M = methyl. Draw the stereocenter with H at the back and OH at the top, as in the figure: the faster enantiomer has OH → cyclohexyl → methyl clockwise, so the cyclohexyl goes at the lower right and the methyl at the lower left. By CIP, OH (O) outranks both carbons; the cyclohexyl carbon carries (C, C, H) and the methyl carbon (H, H, H), so cyclohexyl is 2 and methyl 3. Clockwise is R: the rule predicts that CAL-B acylates (R)-1-cyclohexylethanol faster, leaving (S)-1-cyclohexylethanol behind.

  • Find L and M; put the H away from you; the fast enantiomer has OH → L → M clockwise.
  • Why: M fits the enzyme’s small slot, L has to point out into the open.
  • That shape is (R) when L outranks M by CIP; check the ranks every time.
  • Groups alike in size: a low E.

Check yourself

  1. Which group goes in CAL-B’s small slot?
    Answer

    The medium group, M.

  2. Why is the rule’s shape not always (R)?
    Answer

    R and S follow the CIP ranks, not size. If the medium group outranks the large one, the same shape is named (S).

  3. How will you know, in the lab, which enantiomer your enzyme preferred?
    Answer

    From the chiral GC: name the alcohol’s two peaks with the pure (R) standard, then see which one shrinks as the reaction runs (and which acetate peak grows). Then compare with the rule.

  4. Two alcohols: in one, L is a phenyl and M a methyl; in the other, L is an ethyl and M a methyl. Which gives the larger E, and why?
    Answer

    The first. Phenyl and methyl differ greatly in size, so only one enantiomer fits the slot comfortably; ethyl and methyl are close in size, so both fit nearly as well and the enzyme hardly chooses.

8Filtering through Celite

A yeast broth is full of cells and debris fine enough to clog filter paper in minutes. A bed of Celite catches them in its depth, and the liquid keeps flowing.

Celite is diatomaceous earth: the silica skeletons of tiny single-celled algae, mined, dried and milled into a white powder. Each grain is a hollow, holed shell, and a layer of them packs into a bed full of twisting channels. Liquid passes; particles are caught along the way, throughout the bed’s depth, rather than piling up on one surface and sealing it. That is depth filtration, and it is why a Celite bed can take a broth that would block plain paper at once.

Suction makes it fast. The Büchner funnel sits in a rubber adapter on a thick-walled filter flask; a pump lowers the pressure inside the flask, and the air pressing on the liquid above pushes it through the bed. The flask is clamped, because a funnel full of liquid makes it top-heavy, and a trap stands between the flask and the pump so that liquid sucked over by accident ends up in the trap, not in the pump.

How to do it

  1. Fit the paper to the funnel, wet it so it lies flat, and turn the suction on.
  2. Stir Celite into water to make a thin slurry, pour it onto the paper, and let the suction pack it into a bed 2 to 3 mm thick. Empty the filter flask of that water, or it will dilute your filtrate.
  3. Stir a little more Celite into the broth itself, then pour the broth onto the bed without disturbing it. The cells stay in the bed; the clear filtrate, with your product dissolved in it, collects in the flask.

Celite dust is fine crystalline silica, which scars the lungs if breathed over time. Handle it wet, never sweep it up dry, and keep the lid on the jar.

  • Celite catches fine solids throughout its depth, so the bed does not clog.
  • Build the bed from a slurry under suction, then empty the flask before the broth goes on.
  • Clamp the flask; a trap before the pump; keep the Celite wet.

Check yourself

  1. Why empty the filter flask after building the bed?
    Answer

    So the water from the slurry does not dilute the filtrate that holds your product.

  2. What does the trap protect, and from what?
    Answer

    The pump, from liquid sucked over out of the filter flask.

  3. Why does plain filter paper clog with a yeast broth?
    Answer

    The cells are small enough to lodge in the paper’s pores and build up on its one surface, sealing it.

9Salting out and extracting

Your product is dissolved in a large volume of water. An extraction moves it into a small volume of an organic solvent that does not mix with water, and salt makes the move more complete.

Why salt first

A small alcohol with an ester group likes water a good deal, and a good share of it would stay in the water however much solvent you shook it with. Dissolving salt into the water to saturation changes that. The ions surround themselves with water molecules and leave fewer free to dissolve anything else, so the organic compound is pushed out into the organic layer: salting out. The salt also makes the water layer denser and sharpens the line between the layers.

In the separatory funnel

Pour the filtrate and the ethyl acetate into the funnel, stopper it, and invert it gently several times, holding the stopper in. Vent after every few inversions, with the stopcock up and the stem pointing away from everyone: shaking warms the solvent a little and its vapour builds up pressure. Set the funnel in its ring, take the stopper out, and let the layers part. Then open the stopcock and let the lower layer run out into a flask, closing it just as the interface reaches the stopcock. Keep every layer until you are certain which one holds your product.

Which layer is which, and why the organic layer is on top here

The denser liquid sinks. Water is 1.00 g/mL; saturated salt water about 1.2. Ethyl acetate is 0.90 g/mL, so it floats, and in this lab the organic layer is the top one. That is the case for most common extraction solvents (diethyl ether 0.71, hexane 0.66, ethyl acetate 0.90), but not for the chlorinated ones: dichloromethane (1.33) and chloroform (1.48) sink, and with them the organic layer is the bottom one. Never assume: the test is to add a few drops of water and see which layer they join.

Why three times

Each shake lets the product share itself between the two layers in a fixed ratio. Suppose each extraction takes out 80% of what is left in the water. One extraction with 150 mL takes out a little more than 80%; three with 50 mL each take out 80%, then 80% of the remaining 20%, then 80% of the last 4%: 99.2% in all. Several small extractions always beat one large one.

Emulsions

A broth full of cell debris, protein and fat readily forms an emulsion when shaken hard: tiny droplets of one liquid suspended in the other, a cloudy band where the sharp line should be. Gentle inversions prevent it. If one forms, add brine (the extra salt breaks the droplets), wait, or spin it in a centrifuge; in the lab each of those works.

Fine print

Ethyl acetate and water are not perfectly immiscible: about 8 g of ethyl acetate dissolves in 100 mL of water, and about 3 g of water in 100 mL of ethyl acetate. The first is why some solvent is lost to the water layer; the second is why the organic layer has to be washed and dried before the solvent is evaporated (topic 10).

  • Salt to saturation first: it pushes the product out of the water and sharpens the layers.
  • Invert gently and vent; drain the lower layer out through the stopcock; keep every layer.
  • Ethyl acetate (0.90 g/mL) floats on salt water (1.2): here the organic layer is on top. Chlorinated solvents sink.
  • Three small extractions beat one large one. Hard shaking makes an emulsion.

Check yourself

  1. Which layer is the ethyl acetate, and what do you do with the other?
    Answer

    The upper one. The lower (water) layer is drained out through the stopcock into a flask and kept until you are sure your product is in the organic layer; then it goes back into the funnel for the next extraction.

  2. You switch to dichloromethane. Which layer is now the organic one?
    Answer

    The bottom one: dichloromethane (1.33 g/mL) is denser than even salt water.

  3. Why is the funnel vented, and where should the stem point?
    Answer

    Shaking builds up solvent vapour pressure; vent with the stopcock up and the stem pointing away from everyone.

  4. Why does salt help a water-loving product into the organic layer?
    Answer

    The ions tie up water molecules, leaving fewer to dissolve the product, which is pushed out into the organic layer.

10Drying and evaporating

An organic extract carries dissolved water out of the funnel with it. It has to be taken out before the solvent is evaporated, or the product comes out wet.

Drying

A brine wash comes first: shaking the organic layer with saturated salt water pulls most of its dissolved water back into the water layer, for the same reason salting out works. Then a drying agent takes the rest. Anhydrous magnesium sulfate binds water into its crystals as a hydrate. It is added in small portions with swirling: the first portions clump as they take up water; when a fresh portion stays loose and free-flowing, like dry sand, the solution is dry. Too much drying agent is wasteful and holds back product on its surface, which is why the brine wash comes first. The solid is filtered off by gravity and rinsed with a little fresh solvent, and the filtrate is collected in a round-bottomed flask that was weighed empty.

Evaporating

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure reaches the pressure above it. Lower that pressure and it boils at a lower temperature: water boils at 100 °C in the open and at about 45 °C under the vacuum of a water aspirator. The rotary evaporator uses that to take a solvent off at a temperature that cannot harm the product.

Water droplets left in the flask after the solvent has gone mean the solution was not dry: dissolve the residue again, dry it properly and evaporate again. A product that is itself fairly volatile (one that boils only a little above the solvent) is lost into the receiving flask unless the bath is kept cold and the vacuum gentle; the lab warns you when your compound is one of those.

  • Brine wash, then MgSO4 in portions until a portion stays free-flowing.
  • Gravity filter into a weighed flask; rinse the solid.
  • Under vacuum the solvent boils at a low temperature; the turning flask gives a thin film and no bumping.
  • Droplets left: not dry. A volatile product: cold bath, gentle vacuum.

Check yourself

  1. How do you know you have added enough MgSO4?
    Answer

    New portions stay loose and free-flowing, like sand, instead of clumping.

  2. Why does the rotary evaporator need a vacuum at all? The bath could just be hotter.
    Answer

    A hotter bath would decompose or evaporate many products, and the ethyl acetate would boil violently. The vacuum lets the solvent boil at about room temperature instead.

  3. Why is the flask turned?
    Answer

    To spread the liquid in a thin film, which evaporates fast, and to stop it bumping (boiling in sudden bursts that throw liquid up the duct).

11TLC and the flash column

Both put a mixture onto silica and wash it along with a solvent. On a plate it shows you how far each compound moves; in a column it lets you collect them one at a time.

Why some compounds stick

Silica gel is a very fine, porous sand whose surface is covered in Si–OH groups: it is polar, and it hydrogen-bonds. A compound with an OH of its own clings to it; an ester with no OH clings far less. The solvent (the eluent) competes: the more polar it is, the better it pulls compounds off the silica and the farther everything moves. So on silica an alcohol runs well behind its own acetate, and how far behind depends on the eluent.

TLC

A thin-layer chromatography plate is a strip coated with silica. Spot the mixture near the bottom, stand the plate in a jar with a little eluent, and let the solvent climb. Each compound’s Rf is the distance its spot moved divided by the distance the solvent front moved: 0 means it never left the start, 1 that it ran with the front. Aryl compounds show as dark spots under UV light (254 nm) on plates with an indicator; simple alkyl alcohols and esters do not absorb UV and need a stain (vanillin or permanganate, then heat) to show them.

A TLC is how the column’s eluent is chosen. Aim for the slower compound (the alcohol) at Rf about 0.2 to 0.35 with a clear gap to the faster one. Too polar an eluent (1 : 1) runs both near the front, close together, and the column gives mixed fractions; too weak (hexane alone) leaves the alcohol at the start, and on the column it never comes off.

The flash column

The same separation, scaled up: silica packed in a glass column over a plug of cotton and a layer of sand, the mixture loaded at the top in as little solvent as possible, the eluent pushed through with a little air pressure (“flash” is this pressure; it makes the run take minutes rather than hours). The acetate comes off first, then nothing for a while, then the alcohol. Fractions are collected in test tubes, each checked by TLC, and the pure ones for each compound combined and evaporated.

Choosing an eluent (made up)

Compound V’s alcohol and its acetate (made up) on silica, in hexanes : ethyl acetate.

  • Silica is polar: an OH sticks to it, an ester much less. A more polar eluent moves everything farther.
  • Rf = spot distance / solvent-front distance. Aryl compounds show under UV; others need a stain.
  • Choose the eluent by TLC: the alcohol at Rf 0.2 to 0.35, the acetate well ahead.
  • On the column the acetate comes off first; check the fractions by TLC before combining them.

Check yourself

  1. In the made-up example, which eluent would you choose for the column, and which compound comes off first?
    Answer

    9 : 1 puts the alcohol near Rf 0.25 with the acetate well ahead; the acetate comes off first.

  2. Why does the alcohol run behind its acetate on silica?
    Answer

    Its OH hydrogen-bonds to the silica’s Si–OH groups; the acetate has no OH and is held far less.

  3. Your alcohol has no benzene ring. How will you see its spots?
    Answer

    It will not show under UV; dip or spray the plate with a stain (vanillin or permanganate) and warm it.

12Reading an NMR for purity

A 1H NMR spectrum confirms that you made what you meant to, and it shows what else is in the flask. Comparing integrals gives the amount of each.

Each set of equivalent hydrogens in a molecule gives a signal at its own chemical shift (in ppm: hydrogens next to oxygen come at 3.5 to 4.5, those on a plain CH3 near 1). The signal is split by the hydrogens on the neighbouring carbon, n of them giving n + 1 lines. Its integral (the area under it) is proportional to how many hydrogens it holds.

Another compound in the sample gives its own signals beside yours. To compare amounts, divide each integral by the number of hydrogens that signal stands for; the results are in the ratio of the moles of the two compounds. To turn a mole ratio into a mass ratio, multiply each by its molar mass. That mass fraction is what corrects a polarimeter reading for an impurity (topic 2).

Two things the spectrum cannot do: it cannot tell the two enantiomers apart (topic 1), and it cannot see a compound that has no hydrogens. Water in a wet sample does show, as a broad hump.

Worked example (made up)

Compound X’s CH2 (2 H) integrates to 2.00; a singlet from compound Y’s CH3 (3 H) integrates to 0.30. Per hydrogen: X 2.00 / 2 = 1.00, Y 0.30 / 3 = 0.10. So Y is 0.10 / (1.00 + 0.10) = 9.1 mol% of the mixture. If X is 150 g/mol and Y 100 g/mol, by mass Y is (0.10 × 100) / (1.00 × 150 + 0.10 × 100) = 6.3%.

  • Shift says what kind of hydrogen; splitting says how many neighbours; the integral says how many hydrogens.
  • Integral per hydrogen gives the mole ratio of two compounds; molar masses turn it into a mass ratio.
  • It shows impurities and water, never the ee.

Check yourself

  1. A singlet (3 H) integrates to 0.15 beside your compound’s 2 H signal at 2.00. Mole percent of the impurity?
    Answer

    0.15 / 3 = 0.05 per hydrogen against 1.00: 0.05 / 1.05 = 4.8 mol%.

  2. Can this NMR tell you your product’s ee?
    Answer

    No: both enantiomers give the same spectrum (topic 1). It tells you what else is there.

  3. A CH3 next to a CH gives what kind of signal?
    Answer

    A doublet: one neighbouring hydrogen, so 1 + 1 = 2 lines.

13Safety

  • Hexane, ethyl acetate, vinyl acetate are highly flammable: no flames or hot plates near open solvent, and vinyl acetate (which also irritates eyes and nose) stays in the hood. Hexane harms the nerves with long exposure: cap the bottle.
  • Chloroform (for the polarimeter) is toxic and a suspected carcinogen: make up solutions in the hood, with gloves, and rinse the polarimeter tube in the hood too.
  • Celite and silica gel are fine silica dusts that harm the lungs: keep them wet, or handle them in the hood.
  • Novozym 435 is a respiratory sensitizer (enzyme dust can bring on asthma): weigh the beads gently, without making dust.
  • Vacuum glassware (the filter flask, the rotary evaporator’s flasks) can implode: check for cracks and stars, and clamp the filter flask.
  • The separatory funnel builds pressure when shaken: vent it, stem away from everyone, and never point a stoppered funnel at anyone.
