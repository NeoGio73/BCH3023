Part A

Mirror images, measured

Two molecules can have the same atoms, joined the same way, and still differ as your two hands do. This part is about telling them apart and measuring how much of each a sample holds.

1Enantiomers and enantiomeric excess

A carbon with four different groups is a stereocenter: its two mirror-image arrangements cannot be laid on top of each other.

The two mirror images of a chiral molecule are its enantiomers. They have the same boiling point, the same NMR spectrum in an ordinary solvent, the same TLC spot: in a world without chirality they are identical. They differ only toward other chiral things: polarized light (topic 2), a chiral column (topic 3), and enzymes, whose pockets are built from L-amino acids and are themselves chiral.

Each stereocenter is named R or S by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) rules: rank the four groups by atomic number at the first point of difference (OH > carbon groups > H for the alcohols in this lab), point the lowest (H) away from you, and follow 1 → 2 → 3. Clockwise is R, anticlockwise S. The letter is a naming rule, not a shape: change one group’s rank and the same shape can change its letter.

A 50 : 50 mixture is racemic. How far a sample is from racemic is its enantiomeric excess:

ee = (major − minor) / (major + minor) × 100%An 80 : 20 mixture has ee = (80 − 20) / 100 = 60%. A pure enantiomer has 100% ee; a racemate 0%.

The same thing is sometimes written as an enantiomeric ratio, er 80 : 20. Both are only as good as the method that measured them, which is what the next two topics are about.

From a ratio to ee, and back

  • Enantiomers differ only toward chiral things: light, a chiral column, an enzyme.
  • R and S come from the CIP ranks of the groups, so they are a label, not a shape.
  • ee = (major − minor) / (major + minor).

Check yourself

  1. A sample is 92 : 8. What is its ee?
    Answer

    (92 − 8) / 100 = 84% ee.

  2. A sample has 50% ee. What share of it is the minor enantiomer?
    Answer

    25%: the mixture is 75 : 25, since (75 − 25) / 100 = 0.50.

  3. Why can an ordinary NMR spectrum not tell you the ee?
    Answer

    In an achiral solvent the two enantiomers have identical spectra: every signal is the same for both.

2Optical rotation and the polarimeter

A solution of one enantiomer turns the plane of polarized light; its mirror image turns it by the same angle the other way.

A polarimeter shines light of one wavelength, usually the sodium D line at 589 nm, through a polarizer, then through a tube of the solution, then measures the angle α through which the plane of polarization has turned. A digital instrument finds the angle itself and shows it; an older manual one has you turn an analyzer until the two halves of a field look alike, and read a scale.

The angle grows with how many molecules the light passes, so it is divided by the tube’s length and the concentration to give a property of the compound, the specific rotation:

[α] = α / (l · c)α in degrees, l in decimetres (a standard tube is 100 mm = 1 dm), c in g per mL. Tables quote c in g per 100 mL (“c 1.0” means 1 g in 100 mL, which is 0.010 g per mL), with the solvent and the temperature, since [α] changes with both.

For a sample of one compound whose pure enantiomer’s [α] is known, the ratio of the two gives the optical purity, which is taken as the ee: ee ≈ [α]sample / [α]pure.

What goes wrong

  • No blank. The instrument’s zero is set with the tube full of the pure solvent. Skip it and every reading carries the instrument’s own offset.
  • An air bubble in the light path scatters the light and the reading is wrong. Fill the tube slowly, tilted, and look along it.
  • Something else in the sample. An impurity that does not rotate still counts in the weighed mass, so c is too high for the compound that does rotate and [α] comes out low. If you know what fraction of the mass is your compound (from an NMR, topic 12), divide by it.
  • Small angles. At c 1.0 in a 1 dm tube, a compound with [α] = +40° turns the light only +0.40°: a digital instrument reads that to a few thousandths of a degree, an eye on a scale to about a twentieth.

A made-up polarimeter reading

Compound Q (made up): its pure (+) enantiomer has [α] = +60.0° (c 1.0, chloroform, 25 °C). Enter your own reading and amounts.

Worked example (made up)

248 mg of Q made up to 25.0 mL is 0.00992 g per mL. In a 1 dm tube it reads α = +0.512°. [α] = 0.512 / (1 × 0.00992) = +51.6°, so ee ≈ 51.6 / 60.0 = 86%. If an NMR showed the sample was 95% Q by mass, the ee of the Q itself is 86 / 0.95 = 91%.

  • [α] = α / (l c), l in dm, c in g/mL.
  • ee ≈ [α] / [α]pure, for the same solvent and temperature.
  • Zero on a blank; no bubbles; correct for anything else in the weighed sample.

Check yourself

  1. c is written “c 2.0”. What is it in g per mL?
    Answer

    2.0 g per 100 mL = 0.020 g per mL.

  2. You forget the blank and the instrument reads +0.05° with nothing in it. What happens to a sample reading of +0.40°?
    Answer

    It reads about +0.45°, 12% too high, and so does the ee worked from it.

  3. Why would a manual polarimeter be a poor choice for a sample that turns only +0.40°?
    Answer

    Reading a scale by eye to about ±0.05° puts the ee out by over 10%.

3Chiral gas chromatography

A column coated with a chiral stationary phase holds one enantiomer a little longer than the other, so they come out as two peaks.

The chiral GC columns used for small alcohols and esters carry a cyclodextrin: a ring of seven glucose units (for β-cyclodextrin) with a cone-shaped cavity, its hydroxyl groups capped with methyl or acetyl groups. Each enantiomer fits the chiral cavity a little differently, so one spends a little longer in the coating and comes off later.

The detector (a flame ionization detector, FID) gives a peak for each compound; the integrator reports each peak’s retention time and area. The two enantiomers burn alike, so their areas are in the ratio of their amounts and the ee comes straight from them: ee = |A1 − A2| / (A1 + A2).

Which peak is which?

Nothing in the chromatogram says which peak is R. The order depends on the compound and on the column: the same pair can come off R first on one cyclodextrin and S first on another. The only sure way is a standard: a pure (or nearly pure) sample of one known enantiomer, run the same way. Its peak’s time names the peak in your sample.

What goes wrong

  • Too much. An undiluted sample overloads the column: the peaks broaden and lean, and two close peaks run into one, which the integrator reports as a single peak. Dilute until the peaks are sharp.
  • Solid in the vial. Anything that keeps reacting (a catalyst, a bead of enzyme) must be filtered out first, or the vial is not what the reaction was when you sampled it.

A made-up chromatogram and its standard

Compound W (made up) on a made-up chiral column. Point at a peak to see the integrator’s numbers; switch between the sample and the pure (S)-W standard.

Point at a peak.

  • Peak areas give the ee; the integrator gives the areas.
  • Name the peaks from a standard, run the same way. Never from a remembered order.
  • Dilute the sample and filter out anything that keeps reacting.

Check yourself

  1. Peaks at 6.10 and 6.42 min have areas 12 450 and 2 050. What is the ee?
    Answer

    (12 450 − 2 050) / 14 500 = 71.7%.

  2. In the made-up chromatogram above, which enantiomer is the major one?
    Answer

    Run the standard: (S)-W comes off with the first peak, and the first peak is the small one. The major enantiomer is (R)-W.

  3. An undiluted sample shows one broad peak where a diluted one shows two. What ee would you get from the undiluted one, and why is it wrong?
    Answer

    100%, since there is only one peak to measure; the two enantiomers have run together, not become one.

Part B

Enzymes as reagents

An enzyme’s pocket is chiral, so it treats mirror-image molecules differently. Session 1 uses that to make one enantiomer from a flat ketone; session 2 uses it to sort the two enantiomers of an alcohol.

4Baker’s yeast reduces a ketone

Living yeast cells carry reductases that hand a hydride from NADPH to a ketone’s carbonyl from one face, so the new alcohol comes out mostly as one enantiomer.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is used here as a bag of enzymes that looks after itself. Fed sugar, the cells make NADPH (largely through the pentose phosphate pathway), and several NADPH-dependent ketoreductases use it to reduce a ketone added to the broth. The cells regenerate the NADPH as long as they have sugar, which is why a fermentation is fed, and why the reduction needs days rather than minutes.

A ketone’s carbonyl carbon is flat: hydride can arrive from either face. In a reductase’s pocket the ketone sits one way round, so hydride comes from one face, and a new stereocenter forms with one configuration. A general pattern for many yeast reductases (Prelog’s rule) is that the hydride arrives on the carbonyl’s Re face, which for most simple ketones gives the (S) alcohol; but several enzymes compete in one cell, and some prefer the other face, which is why yeast products are rarely 100% one enantiomer. How the cells are fed and how much ketone they meet at once change which enzymes do most of the work.

The bench, in outline

  1. Wake the yeast in warm water with sugar (yeast likes about 30 to 40 °C; water near 55 °C or hotter kills it). A live culture foams.
  2. Add the ketone, stir open to the air, and wait; follow it by TLC until the ketone’s spot is gone.
  3. Filter off the cells through Celite (topic 8), salt the filtrate and extract the product (topic 9), dry and evaporate (topic 10), weigh, and measure its rotation (topic 2) and NMR (topic 12).

  • Whole cells: the enzymes, and the sugar that keeps their NADPH coming.
  • One face of the flat carbonyl gets the hydride: a new stereocenter, mostly one enantiomer.
  • Several reductases compete, so the ee depends on how the fermentation is run.

Check yourself

  1. Why does a yeast reduction need sugar, though sugar is not reduced?
    Answer

    The cells burn it to regenerate the NADPH the reductases use up.

  2. The water for the yeast was 70 °C. What will you see, and what will the ketone do?
    Answer

    No foam: the yeast is dead, and the ketone is not reduced.

5A lipase in hexane

Lipases are made to hydrolyse fats in water; in a dry organic solvent the same machinery runs the other way and makes esters.

Candida antarctica lipase B (CAL-B) is sold adsorbed on acrylic resin beads as Novozym 435. Its active site is a catalytic triad: a serine (Ser105), a histidine (His224) and an aspartate (Asp187). The histidine takes the serine’s proton, so the serine’s oxygen attacks an ester’s carbonyl; the alkoxy group leaves and an acyl-enzyme remains. Then a nucleophile takes the acyl group off the serine: water in water (hydrolysis), or, in a dry solvent, an alcohol, which is how the alcohol becomes an ester.

With vinyl acetate as the acyl donor, the group that leaves the acyl-enzyme step is vinyl alcohol, which at once turns into its tautomer, acetaldehyde. Acetaldehyde cannot attack the acyl-enzyme, so nothing runs back: the acylation is one-way.

Water spoils this. In wet solvent the acyl-enzyme is hydrolysed as well as transferred, and the ester already made can be hydrolysed back. The hexane is dried, and the beads are kept dry and cold.

R–OH + CH2=CH–O–C(O)CH3 → R–O–C(O)CH3 + CH3CHOAn alcohol and vinyl acetate give the alcohol’s acetate and acetaldehyde, by way of the acyl-enzyme.

  • Ser–His–Asp: the serine is acylated, then deacylated by the alcohol.
  • Vinyl acetate frees vinyl alcohol, which becomes acetaldehyde: one-way.
  • Dry solvent, or the lipase hydrolyses.

Check yourself

  1. Why would ethyl acetate be a worse acyl donor than vinyl acetate?
    Answer

    It frees ethanol, which can attack the acyl-enzyme too: the reaction runs both ways and stops at an equilibrium.

  2. What does the histidine of the triad do?
    Answer

    It takes the serine’s proton (helped by the aspartate), making the serine oxygen a strong nucleophile.

6Kinetic resolution and E

If an enzyme acylates one enantiomer of a racemic alcohol much faster than the other, stopping halfway leaves one enantiomer as the alcohol and the other as the acetate.

Start with a racemic alcohol. The fast enantiomer turns into its acetate; the slow one mostly stays. As the reaction runs, the alcohol left is enriched in the slow enantiomer (its ee, ees, rises) and the acetate made is mostly the fast one (its ee, eep). At most 50% of the starting material can become the fast enantiomer’s acetate, so a resolution is stopped near 50% conversion, c. Going on past that converts the slow enantiomer too: the alcohol left gets purer still, and the acetate less pure.

How well the enzyme tells the two apart is its enantioselectivity, E: the ratio of how fast it handles each (strictly, the ratio of the two kcat/KM). Because the two enantiomers compete for the same enzyme, E links c and the ee values whatever the time scale (Chen, Fujimoto, Girdaukas and Sih, 1982):

c = ees / (ees + eep)From the two ee values alone (as fractions); no masses needed.

E = ln[(1 − c)(1 − ees)] / ln[(1 − c)(1 + ees)]c and ees as fractions (0.40, not 40).

E above about 100 is excellent; below about 15, neither product comes out pure. Above about 200 the formula is very sensitive to the last digit of the ee values, so a large E is a range, not a number.

How E shapes a resolution

The ee of the alcohol left (blue) and of the acetate made (orange) as the conversion rises, for the E you choose.

Worked example (made up)

A resolution stopped and separated gives the alcohol at ees = 62% and the acetate at eep = 93%. c = 0.62 / (0.62 + 0.93) = 0.400, so 40%. E = ln[(0.600)(0.380)] / ln[(0.600)(1.620)] = ln 0.228 / ln 0.972 = −1.478 / −0.0284 = about 52.

  • Fast enantiomer → acetate; slow one stays as the alcohol. Stop near 50%.
  • c from the two ee values; E from c and ees.
  • A large E is a range: the formula swings with the last digit.

Check yourself

  1. ees = 30%, eep = 90%. What is c?
    Answer

    0.30 / 1.20 = 25%.

  2. Why does the acetate’s ee fall if you run far past 50%?
    Answer

    Once the fast enantiomer is used up, the slow one is acylated too, and its acetate dilutes the fast one’s.

  3. With E = 5, can you get both products pure by stopping at exactly 50%?
    Answer

    No. Use the plot: at E = 5 and 50%, both ee values are only about 50%.

7The Kazlauskas rule

For secondary alcohols, many lipases (CAL-B among them) prefer the enantiomer that fits their pocket with its larger group in the larger space.

A secondary alcohol’s stereocenter carries an OH, an H, and two carbon groups, one larger (L) and one smaller, the medium group (M). Kazlauskas and co-workers (1991) found that lipases acylate faster the enantiomer in which, with the H pointing away from you, OH → L → M runs clockwise. CAL-B’s crystal structure shows why: its stereospecificity pocket is small, so it takes the medium group, and the large group must point out of the active site.

If L also outranks M by the CIP rules, that faster enantiomer is the (R) one. If M outranks L (a medium group carrying a heavier atom, or a double or triple bond, can outrank a longer alkyl chain), the same shape gets the other letter. Always work out the letter from the ranks; do not assume it.

The rule is about sizes, so it works best when L and M differ a lot. When they are alike in size (one carbon apart, say), the enzyme can hardly tell the enantiomers apart and E is low.

Worked example: 1-cyclohexylethanol (not one of the lab’s alcohols)

L = cyclohexyl, M = methyl. Draw the stereocenter with H at the back: the faster enantiomer has OH → cyclohexyl → methyl clockwise. By CIP, OH > cyclohexyl (a carbon bearing C, C, H) > methyl (H, H, H), so clockwise is R: the rule predicts that CAL-B acylates (R)-1-cyclohexylethanol faster, leaving (S)-1-cyclohexylethanol behind.

  • L and M; H away; the fast one has OH → L → M clockwise.
  • That is (R) when L outranks M by CIP; check the ranks every time.
  • Groups alike in size: low E.

Check yourself

  1. Which group goes in CAL-B’s small pocket?
    Answer

    The medium group, M.

  2. Why is the rule’s shape not always (R)?
    Answer

    R and S follow the CIP ranks, not size. If the medium group outranks the large one, the same shape is named (S).

  3. How will you know, in the lab, which enantiomer your enzyme preferred?
    Answer

    From the chiral GC: name the alcohol’s peaks with the pure (R) standard, then see which one shrinks as the reaction runs (and which acetate peak grows). Then compare with the rule.

At the bench

8Filtering through Celite

A yeast broth is full of cells and debris fine enough to clog filter paper in minutes. Celite (diatomaceous earth: the silica skeletons of tiny algae) packs into a porous bed that holds the fine solid in its depth rather than on the paper’s surface. Make a slurry of Celite in water, pour it onto the paper in the Büchner funnel under suction to form a bed 2 to 3 mm thick, empty the filter flask, then filter the broth (with more Celite stirred into it) through the bed.

The filter flask is clamped (it is top-heavy under a funnel), and a trap sits between it and the pump so that liquid cannot be pulled into the pump. Celite dust is crystalline silica and harms the lungs: handle it wet and never sweep it dry.

Check yourself

  1. Why empty the filter flask after building the bed?
    Answer

    So the water from the slurry does not dilute the filtrate that holds your product.

9Salting out and extracting

A small alcohol with an ester group likes water a good deal, so extracting it straight from the filtrate leaves much behind. Dissolving salt to saturation in the water first (salting out) ties up the water molecules around the ions and pushes the organic compound into the organic layer.

In the separatory funnel, add the organic solvent, stopper, invert gently and vent often (pointing the stem away from everyone), then let the layers part and drain the lower one. Ethyl acetate is less dense than water and floats; a chlorinated solvent would sink. Keep every layer until you are sure where your product is. Three extractions with a third of the solvent each recover more than one with all of it.

A broth full of cell debris readily forms an emulsion when shaken hard: no sharp line between the layers. Gentle inversions prevent it; brine (saturated NaCl), patience, or a centrifuge clear it.

Check yourself

  1. Which layer is ethyl acetate, and why does it matter which you drain?
    Answer

    The upper one (it is less dense than water). Draining the lower layer through the stopcock removes the water and keeps your extract in the funnel.

10Drying and evaporating

An organic extract holds dissolved water. A brine wash first takes out most of it; then anhydrous magnesium sulfate is added in small portions with swirling until fresh portions stay free-flowing instead of clumping. Filter off the solid by gravity, rinse it, and collect the filtrate in a weighed round-bottomed flask.

The rotary evaporator takes off the solvent under reduced pressure while the flask turns in a warm bath: the vacuum lowers the boiling point, the turning spreads a thin film, and the condenser catches the solvent. Droplets left behind mean the solution was not dry. A small, volatile product (one that boils not far above 100 °C) is lost along with the solvent if the bath is warm and the vacuum strong: use a cold bath and gentle vacuum for those.

Check yourself

  1. How do you know you have added enough MgSO4?
    Answer

    New portions stay loose and free-flowing, like sand, instead of clumping.

11TLC and the flash column

On silica, polar compounds stick and travel less far. An alcohol, which can hydrogen-bond to the silica through its OH, runs well behind its own acetate. TLC tells you how far each goes in a solvent (its Rf, distance run over the solvent front’s), and so which solvent will separate them on a column: aim for the slower compound at Rf about 0.2 to 0.35, with a clear gap to the faster one. Too polar a solvent runs both near the front, close together; too weak a solvent leaves the slower one at the start.

Aryl compounds show as dark spots under UV light (254 nm) on plates with an indicator; most simple alkyl alcohols and esters do not, and need a stain (vanillin or permanganate, then heat).

A flash column is the same separation, scaled up: silica packed in a glass column, the mixture loaded at the top, solvent pushed through with a little air pressure. The faster compound (here the acetate) comes off first. Fractions are collected in test tubes, checked by TLC, and the pure ones combined.

12Reading an NMR for purity

A 1H NMR spectrum confirms a structure: each set of equivalent protons gives a signal at its chemical shift, split by its neighbours (n neighbours give n + 1 lines), with an integral proportional to how many protons it holds. It also shows what else is in the sample. Another compound’s signals appear beside yours, and comparing integrals gives the mole ratio, once each is divided by its number of protons.

Worked example (made up)

Compound X’s CH2 (2 H) integrates to 2.00; a singlet from compound Y’s CH3 (3 H) integrates to 0.30. Per proton: X 1.00, Y 0.10. So Y is 0.10 / 1.10 = 9.1 mol% of the mixture. To turn that into a mass fraction, multiply each by its molar mass.

Check yourself

  1. A singlet (3 H) integrates to 0.15 beside your compound’s 2 H signal at 2.00. Mole percent of the impurity?
    Answer

    0.05 / (1.00 + 0.05) = 4.8 mol%.

  2. Can this NMR tell you your product’s ee?
    Answer

    No: both enantiomers give the same spectrum (topic 1). It tells you what else is there.

13Safety

  • Hexane, ethyl acetate, vinyl acetate are highly flammable; vinyl acetate and chloroform belong in the hood. Hexane harms the nerves on long exposure.
  • Chloroform (for the polarimeter) is toxic and a suspected carcinogen: make up solutions in the hood, with gloves.
  • Celite and silica gel are fine dusts that harm the lungs: keep them wet or handle them in the hood.
  • Novozym 435 is a respiratory sensitizer (enzyme dust): weigh the beads gently, without making dust.
  • Vacuum glassware (the filter flask, the rotary evaporator) can implode: check for cracks, and clamp the filter flask.
