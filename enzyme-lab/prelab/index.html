1The enzyme, and why anyone cares

Corn stalks, wood chips and grass are mostly cellulose: long chains of glucose, the sugar that yeast can ferment into ethanol fuel. The hard part is getting the glucose out. Cellulose is tough, and it takes a team of enzymes, proteins that each speed up one particular chemical reaction, to take it apart.

Other enzymes on the team cut cellulose down to cellobiose, which is two glucose units joined together. Cellobiase, the enzyme in this lab, finishes the job: it splits each cellobiose into two molecules of glucose. The cellobiase you'll use comes from a fungus, Aspergillus niger.

Two words appear all through the lab. The substrate is the molecule an enzyme works on. The product is what the enzyme makes from it.

Sources: Bio-Rad manual, U.S. Department of Energy

2The reaction you'll watch

There is a catch: cellobiose and glucose are both colorless, so you can't see cellobiase working. The kit gets around this with a stand-in substrate, p-nitrophenyl glucopyranoside. It is glucose joined to a small molecule called p-nitrophenol. Cellobiase cuts it the way it cuts cellobiose, and each cut sets free one glucose and one p-nitrophenol.

p-Nitrophenol has a useful habit: it turns yellow in a basic solution. That is where the stop solution, a strong base, comes in. When a sample of the reaction goes into it, two things happen at once:

  • the reaction stops, so the sample stays as it was at that moment;
  • the p-nitrophenol in it turns yellow. The more product, the deeper the yellow.

In a real lab the stop solution is handled with goggles and gloves: strong bases burn skin and eyes.

3From yellow to a number

The amount of product is counted in nanomoles (nmol). You'll find it in two ways.

By eye

Five cuvettes, the standards S1 to S5, hold known amounts of p-nitrophenol: 0, 12.5, 25, 50 and 100 nmol. Hold your cuvette beside them, pick the standard whose color is closest, and record that standard's amount. It is quick, but there are only five possible answers.

By spectrophotometer

A spectrophotometer shines violet light (410 nm) through the cuvette and reports the absorbance: how much of that light the solution took in. The deeper the yellow, the higher the absorbance. Before reading anything else you blank the instrument with S1, the standard with no p-nitrophenol in it. That sets its zero.

The standard curve

Read the absorbance of the other four standards too. Plot the five readings against the amounts you already know, and they fall along a straight line: your standard curve. The line now works as a translator. Take any sample's absorbance, go across to the line, then straight down, and read off the amount.

Try it on this example curve. Move the slider, or click on the graph, to set a sample's absorbance. Follow the dashed line across to the curve and down to the amount. These are example readings; in the lab you'll measure your own standards.

4From amounts to a rate

A rate says how fast the product is appearing: nanomoles per minute. The lab asks for the initial rate, the rate at the start of the reaction.

From several timed samples

Plot the amount in each cuvette against the time its sample was taken. Where the points climb in a straight line, the slope of that line is the initial rate: the change in amount divided by the change in time.

In the example graph the amount goes from 9 nmol at 1 minute to 27 nmol at 3 minutes.
(27 − 9) ÷ (3 − 1) = 18 ÷ 2 = 9 nmol per minute

From one sample

Some reactions are sampled only once. Count the start as 0 nmol at 0 minutes, and the rate is the amount divided by the minutes.

A sample taken at 2 minutes holds 22 nmol.
22 ÷ 2 = 11 nmol per minute

6At the virtual bench

  • Each pipet has one job, written on it: Stop for stop solution, Sub for substrate, E for enzyme, and so on. Click a pipet to pick it up, click a tube to draw liquid up, click where it goes, then click the bench to put the pipet back. Keeping one pipet to one job means nothing gets carried from one tube into another.
  • Set how much the pipet draws: 250 µL, 500 µL or 1 mL. One milliliter is 1,000 µL, so 2 mL is 1 mL twice.
  • Stop solution goes into the cuvettes first. Then each sample is stopped the moment it goes in.
  • The timer starts by itself when enzyme and substrate meet. The note under the bench tells you the next step and when each sample is due.
  • Fast forward runs the clock ten times faster, and drops back to normal speed 5 seconds before a sample is due.
  • Blank the spectrophotometer with S1 before you read anything. You read the standards only once: the same standard curve serves all five activities.
  • Made a mistake? "Wash up and set out again" empties the bench so you can run that activity again.
  • Your work is saved in your browser. Use the same browser on the same computer for both pages, so your standard curve is there when you come back.
  • It is a simulation. Its numbers follow the typical classroom results in the kit's manual, with a little scatter, so your results won't be exactly the same as anyone else's.
