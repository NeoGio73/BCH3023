1What you will do

A patient's blood sample has come to the clinic's molecular diagnostics laboratory. The question is about one gene, HBB, which codes for the β chain of hemoglobin: which form of it (which allele) does the patient carry on each of their two copies of chromosome 11? Changes in this gene cause some of the most common inherited diseases in the world, and every patient in the clinic is different.

You will answer the question the way a real laboratory does: copy the part of the gene where the changes lie, test it with an enzyme that cuts only one version, and read its sequence base by base.

Session 1: from a read to a PCR product

  1. Which gene? Identify a read of the patient's DNA with BLAST.
  2. Primers. Design two, test them with Primer-BLAST and the page's rules.
  3. The reaction. Work out every volume for three tubes: patient, control, no DNA.
  4. At the bench. Pipette the tubes, program the thermocycler, run it.
  5. The digest. Set up a restriction digest of the products, and predict the pieces.
  6. For sequencing. Clean up a tube of product and add a sequencing primer.

Session 2: from the products to a diagnosis

  1. The gel. Choose its strength, weigh the agarose, cast it.
  2. Load and run. Ladder, products and digests, with loading dye.
  3. The picture. Photograph the gel on the blue-light box.
  4. The sizes. Measure the ladder, fit a line, size every band.
  5. The sequence. Read the trace, find any double peaks, place them in the gene.
  6. The protein. From the gene to the β chain, and the structure in 3D.
  7. The diagnosis. Both alleles, with the evidence, and the report.

Why more than one method?

Each method answers a different question, and each has blind spots. A diagnosis rests on several that agree.

MethodWhat it showsWhat it cannot show

Every sequence and number you practise on here is made up. None of it is the β-globin gene, and none of it is a patient of the clinic, so nothing on this page tells you what your patient carries. The page checks your readings and calculations against your own results; the alleles and the diagnosis are yours to work out, and your instructor marks them.

2Hemoglobin and its genes

Hemoglobin carries oxygen in red blood cells. Each molecule is made of four chains: in an adult, mostly two α chains (141 amino acids each) and two β chains (146 each), written α2β2. Each chain folds round a heme, a flat ring holding an iron(II) ion, and each heme binds one O2. The four chains work together: binding oxygen at one heme makes it easier at the others, which is why hemoglobin loads oxygen in the lungs and gives it up in the tissues so well.

The α chains are coded on chromosome 16. The β chain and its relatives sit side by side on chromosome 11, in the β-globin locus, in the order in which they are switched on during life:

These genes arose from one ancestral gene by duplication, so they are still alike. The δ chain differs from the β chain in only about ten of its 146 amino acids, and the two genes are nearly the same base for base over long stretches. That matters for you: a primer written for one may also bind the other. Section 6 comes back to this.

Each globin gene has the same plan: three exons (the parts kept in the messenger RNA) separated by two introns (cut out before the message leaves the nucleus).

Two copies, two alleles

Everyone has two copies of chromosome 11, one from each parent, so two copies of HBB. The versions a person carries make up their genotype:

  • Homozygous: both copies the same allele (both normal, or both carrying the same change).
  • Heterozygous: one copy normal, one changed. A person heterozygous for a recessive disease allele is a carrier, and is usually healthy.
  • Compound heterozygous: two different changed alleles, one on each copy. Neither copy is normal.

Many disorders of hemoglobin are recessive: one working copy is enough for good health, and disease appears when neither copy works properly. Whether a given change, on one copy or on both, causes disease, and how severe it is, is what databases such as ClinVar record (section 12).

A primer is designed to copy part of the β-globin gene. Primer-BLAST reports that it would also copy a second product. Which gene is most likely to blame, and why?

The δ-globin gene, a few thousand bases upstream on the same chromosome. It arose by duplication of the same ancestral gene, and over long stretches its sequence is nearly the same as β-globin's, so a primer that fits one can fit the other.

3DNA: strands, ends and complements

A DNA double helix is two strands running in opposite directions. Each strand has a 5′ end (a phosphate on the 5′ carbon of the sugar) and a 3′ end (a free 3′ OH). Across the helix, A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G with C (three). So each strand fixes the other: it is its complement.

By convention, every sequence is written 5′ to 3′, left to right. A reference record such as a GenBank entry gives just one strand, the top strand; the other is understood.

  • The complement swaps each base for its partner (A↔T, G↔C), in place.
  • The reverse complement is the complement read backwards, so that it too runs 5′ to 3′. It is the other strand, written the usual way.

You will need the reverse complement often: for a reverse primer (section 6), for a site on the other strand, and for a sequencing read made from the reverse primer, which reads the bottom strand.

The other strand of any sequence

A reverse complement by hand

A made-up top strand reads, at the far end of a stretch you want to copy, 5′-GATTCCAGTTGCAAGCTGAC-3′. The reverse primer must bind there: it is the reverse complement.

Is the reverse complement of 5′-AAGC-3′ written GCTT or TTCG?

5′-GCTT-3′. The complement in place is TTCG (that is the other strand read 3′ to 5′); written backwards, so that it runs 5′ to 3′, it is GCTT.

4Which gene is this? BLAST

BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, run by NCBI) compares a sequence, your query, with every sequence in a database and lists the best matches. It works fast because it does not try every alignment: it cuts the query into short words, finds exact matches to those words in the database, then extends each match in both directions, adding to a score for matching bases and taking away for mismatches and gaps. Alignments that score well are reported.

  • Description and Scientific name: the gene and the organism the hit comes from.
  • Max score: the score of the best alignment, in bits; higher is better. For blastn it is roughly two bits for every matching base.
  • Query cover: how much of your query the alignment spans.
  • E value: how many hits this good you would expect by chance in a database of this size. The smaller, the surer; a long, perfect match is printed as 0.0. It falls steeply as the score rises (roughly, E ≈ m × n × 2−score, for a query of m bases and a database of n).

  • Accession: the database's name for the record. The letters say what kind of record it is (below).

A good identification has a tiny E value, an identity close to 100% over nearly all of the query, and a description that makes sense. The chimpanzee's copy of a human gene often comes second, at 98 to 99%: our genes are that close. A related gene in the same person (the fourth hit) matches only part of the query, less well.

In the made-up result, the mRNA of the same gene matches the read perfectly (100% identity) but covers only 46% of it. Why?

Part of the read lies in an intron. Introns are spliced out of the mRNA, so the mRNA record holds only the exon parts of the read. The gene's genomic record (NG_) holds the introns too, and matches all of it.

5How PCR copies DNA

The polymerase chain reaction copies one chosen stretch of DNA, out of a whole genome, a billion times over in a couple of hours. It needs:

In the tubeWhat it does
Template DNAThe patient's DNA, which holds the stretch to be copied. A few nanograms are enough: 20 ng of human DNA holds about 6,000 copies of any one gene.
Two primersShort single strands (about 20 bases) that bind either end of the stretch and give the polymerase a place to start. They decide what is copied.
Taq DNA polymeraseA polymerase from a bacterium of hot springs, Thermus aquaticus, which survives the 95 °C steps. It adds nucleotides to the 3′ end of a primer, copying the strand the primer sits on.
dNTPsThe four building blocks, dATP, dCTP, dGTP and dTTP.
Mg2+ and bufferThe polymerase needs magnesium ions to work, and a steady pH and salt.

A master mix holds the polymerase, dNTPs, Mg2+ and buffer together, at twice (2x) or five times (5x) the strength the reaction needs. You add primers, template and water.

One cycle, three steps

  1. Denature, about 95 °C: the two strands come apart. The first denaturation is longer, to separate long genomic DNA completely.
  2. Anneal, usually 50 to 65 °C: the primers find and bind their places, one on each strand. The temperature is set from the primers' melting temperature (section 6).
  3. Extend, 72 °C, Taq's best temperature: the polymerase builds a new strand from each primer. Protocols allow about one minute for every 1,000 bases of product.

After the cycles, a final extension of several minutes lets the polymerase finish every strand, and the block then holds the tubes cold until you take them out.

In the first cycles the new strands run on past the far primer's place, because nothing stops them. But from the third cycle on, strands appear whose two ends are exactly the two primers, and these are the ones that double each cycle. Soon they are almost all there is. The product therefore has a fixed length: from the 5′ end of one primer to the 5′ end of the other, primers included.

Each cycle multiplies the product by (1 + E), where E, the efficiency, is at most 1 (perfect doubling). After n cycles:

copies = starting copies × (1 + E)n

The growth slows and stops, a plateau, when primers and dNTPs run short and the polymerase cannot keep up with so many strands.

How many copies?

Starting from about 6,000 copies (20 ng of human DNA), for a product of 600 bp. A band on a gel needs at least a few nanograms of DNA to be seen.

When a PCR goes wrong

What the gel showsLikely causes
No band in any lane, the ladder fineSomething essential left out (master mix, a primer); annealing far too hot; denaturing too cool to separate the strands; too few cycles; an extension too short for the product.
A faint bandAnnealing a little too hot; too few cycles; too little template.
Extra bands of other sizesAnnealing too cool, so the primers also bind places that only nearly match; a primer that fits a related gene.
A small, fuzzy band below 100 bp, even with no DNAPrimer-dimer: the primers copied each other (section 6).
A product band in the no-DNA tubeContamination: DNA got into a stock or a tube (section 7).
A PCR runs at 100% efficiency. Roughly how many times more product is there after 30 cycles than after 20?

About 1,000 times: ten more doublings is 210 = 1,024. (In practice the reaction may already be near its plateau by 30 cycles.)

6Designing primers

The primers are the one part of a PCR you design yourself, and they decide whether it works. Both are written 5′ to 3′:

  • The forward primer is copied straight from the top strand, at the start of the stretch you want.
  • The reverse primer is the reverse complement of the top strand at the far end (section 3).

The melting temperature

A primer's melting temperature, Tm, is the temperature at which half of it is bound to its target. G–C pairs hold more strongly than A–T pairs, and how strongly a pair holds also depends on its neighbours, which stack on each other along the helix. The best estimates (the nearest-neighbour method of SantaLucia, which Primer-BLAST and the lab use) add up the contribution of every neighbouring pair of bases and correct for the salt, the Mg2+ and the amount of primer. Quick formulas such as 2 °C for every A or T plus 4 °C for every G or C give only a rough idea, often several degrees out.

The annealing temperature is set a few degrees below the lower of the two Tm values. Too hot, and the primers do not stay bound: little or no product. Too cool, and they also stay on places that only nearly match: extra products.

The rules, and why

Look over a primer

    Spot the faults

    A pair of primers works well on most people's DNA, but a patient known to carry two different alleles shows only one of them. What might be wrong with the primers?

    One primer sits on a base that differs between the two alleles, near its 3′ end. It copies the allele it matches and hardly the other (allele dropout). Moving the primer a little away from the varying base fixes it.

    7Setting up the reaction

    Every volume in a reaction comes from one equation. The concentration of a stock times the volume you take of it equals the concentration you want times the final volume:

    C1 × V1 = C2 × V2

    For an amount rather than a concentration (nanograms of DNA, units of enzyme), the volume is the amount wanted divided by the stock's concentration. Water makes up the rest of the volume.

    Worked example (made up): a 40 µL reaction needs a 4x buffer at 1x. V1 = 1 × 40 ÷ 4 = 10 µL. It needs a primer at 0.5 µM from a 20 µM stock: V1 = 0.5 × 40 ÷ 20 = 1 µL. And 15 ng of DNA from a stock of 3 ng/µL: 15 ÷ 3 = 5 µL.

    A made-up recipe

    A 50 µL reaction needs a 5x buffer at 1x, a primer at 0.2 µM from a 10 µM stock, and 10 ng of DNA from a stock of 5 ng/µL.

    Pipettes

    Choose the smallest pipette whose range holds the volume: a pipette is least accurate near the bottom of its range.

    PipetteRangeTips
    P20.2 to 2 µLthe small 10 µL tips
    P202 to 20 µLthe 200 µL tips
    P20020 to 200 µLthe 200 µL tips
    1. Set the volume, and push on a fresh tip.
    2. Press the plunger to the first stop, dip the tip just under the surface, and let the plunger up slowly to draw the liquid.
    3. Touch the tip to the inside of the tube, press to the first stop, then on to the second stop to blow out the last drop.
    4. Eject the tip into the bin. A tip is used for one liquid only.

    Contamination, and why every PCR has controls

    A used tip carries a film of everything it touched. Put it back into a stock and the stock now holds a trace of that, and so does every tube made from that stock afterwards. PCR finds even a handful of copies of DNA, so a fresh tip for every liquid is the rule, and the DNA goes in last. That is also why every run has controls:

    • A no-template control: everything but DNA (water in its place). A band there means something was contaminated, and no band in the other lanes can be trusted.
    • A positive control: DNA whose answer is already known. It shows that the reagents, the program and the gel all work, and what a known result looks like.

    The tubes are kept on ice while you work, so that the polymerase cannot start early on primers bound loosely at room temperature. Then each tube is capped, flicked to mix and given a quick spin to bring every drop down off the walls and the cap.

    The no-template control shows a band the same size as the patient's product. Can the patient's result be used?

    No. Some DNA (from the patient, the control or a product) got into a stock or a tube, so the patient's tube may hold it too. The reaction is set up again with fresh tips and, if need be, fresh stocks.

    8Cutting with a restriction enzyme

    Restriction enzymes come from bacteria, where they cut up the DNA of invading viruses. Each one reads a short recognition site, usually four to eight bases, and cuts both strands there, every time the site appears. Many sites read the same on both strands (5′ to 3′): EcoRI's GAATTC is its own reverse complement. A caret marks where the top strand is cut: G^AATTC. An N in a site means any base.

    One changed base can create a site or destroy one. So a PCR product cut with the right enzyme shows, on a gel, which version a person carries: the pieces of a copy with the site, the uncut length of a copy without it, or both. This is PCR-RFLP (restriction fragment length polymorphism).

    A made-up digest

    HinfI cuts G^ANTC. A made-up PCR product of 240 bp has two HinfI sites, whose cuts fall after base 70 and after base 190.

    A digest that works

    • Its own buffer, at 1x: the right salt and Mg2+ for that enzyme.
    • Enough enzyme. One unit cuts 1 µg of a standard DNA completely in an hour at 37 °C. A few units are plenty for the DNA of a PCR.
    • Its temperature (37 °C for most) and time.

    With too little of any of these, some sites stay uncut: a partial digest, which shows extra bands of the uncut pieces still joined, and can look like a heterozygote. A known control digested beside the patient shows whether the enzyme cut fully. Many enzymes are killed afterwards by heating to 65 or 80 °C.

    When working out fragments, list only the ones large enough to see: pieces under about 50 bp hold little DNA and run off or glow too faintly to read.

    A homozygous person's digest shows the uncut product plus the cut pieces. Name two explanations.

    A partial digest (too little enzyme, the wrong buffer, too little time or the wrong temperature), or the person is in fact heterozygous. The control digest tells them apart: if the control, which should be cut completely, also shows uncut product, the digest was partial.

    9The agarose gel

    DNA's phosphate backbone carries one negative charge per base, so every piece has about the same charge for its size. In an electric field it runs toward the positive electrode (red), and the wells go at the negative end (black): "run to red".

    The gel is a mesh of agarose, a polysaccharide from seaweed. Short pieces slip through it faster than long ones, so the pieces sort by size. A weaker gel has a coarser mesh and separates long DNA; a stronger gel separates short DNA.

    The gel is cast with a stain that binds DNA and glows when blue light shines on it; an amber filter in front of the camera blocks the blue so that only the glow shows. (Safer than the older ethidium bromide under ultraviolet.) A band's brightness depends on how much DNA it holds: a short piece holds less DNA than a long one cut from the same product, so it is fainter.

    AgaroseSeparates best, roughly
    1%500 to 10,000 bp
    1.5%200 to 3,000 bp
    2%100 to 2,000 bp
    3%shorter pieces, about 50 to 1,000 bp; two pieces close in size come apart better

    The percentage is weight per volume: a 1% gel is 1 g of agarose in every 100 mL of buffer.

    Weighing agarose

    Casting, loading and running

    1. Weigh the agarose into the buffer and heat it in the microwave, swirling now and then, until it boils and is completely clear: no floating specks.
    2. Let it cool to about 55 to 60 °C (it then sets in 20 to 30 minutes), add the stain, pour it into the tray with the comb in place, and let it set.
    3. Take out the comb, put the gel in the box with the wells at the black end, and cover it with buffer.
    4. Mix each sample with loading dye: it is dense, so the sample sinks into the well instead of floating away, and coloured, so you can see it go in and watch the run. In a 1% gel its two dyes run about with pieces of a few hundred and a few thousand base pairs. The ladder, a mixture of pieces of known sizes, goes in a lane of its own.
    5. Run at about 5 to 8 volts for every centimetre between the electrodes, until the faster dye is most of the way down. Too long, and small pieces run off the end.

    At a real bench: molten agarose can boil over suddenly when it is moved. Swirl it gently, hold it with a heat-proof glove, and point the flask away from you.

    Reading sizes from the ladder

    Over the useful middle of a gel, the distance a piece runs falls in a straight line with the logarithm of its size. So measure every ladder band from the bottom of the wells, plot log10(size) against distance, fit the best straight line, and read each band of your own from it. Very long and very short pieces bend away from the line, so read bands that lie between ladder bands, not beyond them.

    A two-point ladder

    On another made-up gel, the ladder's 1000 bp band ran 20 mm and its 100 bp band 50 mm. Take a straight line of log10(size) against distance through these two.

    Two pieces that differ by only a few base pairs run together as one band, brighter than either would be alone.

    Which runs further on the same gel: a 150 bp piece or a 450 bp piece? And which band is brighter, if both come from cutting the same product?

    The 150 bp piece runs further: shorter pieces slip through the mesh faster. The 450 bp band is brighter, because each copy of it holds three times as much DNA as a copy of the 150 bp piece.

    10Sanger sequencing

    A gel tells you sizes; sequencing tells you every base. In Sanger sequencing, a polymerase copies the template from a single primer, in a mix that holds the four normal dNTPs and a little of each of four dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs). A ddNTP has no 3′ OH, so once it is added nothing can be joined after it: the strand stops there. Each of the four carries its own fluorescent dye.

    Because the ddNTPs are few, each strand stops at a random place, and among millions of strands there is one stopped at every base. The sequencing core sorts them by length in a thin capillary of gel (the shortest arrives first), and a laser reads the colour of each one's last base as it passes: a trace, or chromatogram, of coloured peaks, one base after another.

    A greenC blueG blackT red

    Cleaning up first

    A PCR product still holds leftover primers and dNTPs. Both would spoil the sequencing: the two PCR primers would start reads from both ends at once, giving two sequences on top of each other, and the extra dNTPs would upset the balance with the ddNTPs. So the product is cleaned up with two enzymes: an exonuclease that eats single-stranded DNA (the leftover primers), and a phosphatase that strips the phosphates off the dNTPs. After about 15 minutes at 37 °C, both are killed by heat (about 15 minutes at 80 °C). Only then does the one sequencing primer go in: added before the heat, the exonuclease would eat it too.

    Reading a trace

    • The first 20 to 40 bases after the primer are poorly read, and the peaks fade and spread after several hundred bases (often 700 to 900).
    • Good peaks are sharp, evenly spaced and stand well above the background. Their heights vary a little with their neighbours; that is normal.
    • A read from the reverse primer reads the bottom strand: turn it into its reverse complement to compare it with the reference. A base that is A/T on the top strand appears as T/A on a reverse read.

    Two copies of the gene, one trace

    A PCR product from a person copies both of their alleles together, so the trace is the two read at once. Four made-up traces of the same stretch:

    Two bases at one placeRYSWKM
    are written with the IUPAC letterA/GC/TG/CA/TG/TA/C

    A change on both copies gives no double peak at all: the trace is clean, only different from the reference. To find it, compare the bases called with the reference sequence, for example with BLAST's "Align two sequences".

    Find the double peak

    A made-up trace. Where does it show a double peak, and of which two bases?

    A forward read shows a clean G where the reference has an A, and no double peak. What are the two possibilities, and how would you tell them apart?

    Either both copies carry the change (homozygous), or it is a mistake in the read. A read from the other primer settles it: a real change shows there too, as C where the reference's bottom strand has T.

    11From gene to protein

    Most human genes are split. The gene is copied into RNA whole; then the introns are spliced out and the exons joined to make the messenger RNA. Almost every intron begins with GT and ends with AG (on the DNA's top strand), and those few bases are where the splicing machinery cuts.

    The ribosome reads the message from the start codon, AUG (ATG in the DNA), three bases at a time. Each three-base codon stands for one amino acid, and a stop codon (TAA, TAG or TGA) ends the chain. The grouping into threes is the reading frame; it is set by the start codon.

    What a change does

    A made-up coding sequence, ATG TCC GAT CTG TGG AAA TGA, codes for Met-Ser-Asp-Leu-Trp-Lys and stop. Changes to it:

    Kind of changeExample (made up)Effect
    SilentCTG → CTCStill Leu: the code has more than one codon for most amino acids. Protein unchanged.
    MissenseGAT → GCTAsp becomes Ala. One amino acid changed; the effect depends on where it is and what it becomes.
    NonsenseTGG → TGATrp becomes a stop: the chain ends early. Little or no working protein is made.
    Frameshiftlose the T of TCCATG CCG ATC TGT GGA AAT GA…: Met-Pro-Ile-Cys-Gly-Asn… Every codon after the change is read wrongly, usually until a stop turns up by chance.
    In-frame deletionlose CTGOne amino acid (Leu) missing; the rest is read correctly. Any loss or gain that is a multiple of three keeps the frame.
    Splice sitean intron's GT → ATThe intron is not cut out properly, or a neighbouring exon is skipped: the message is wrong from there on.
    Inside an introna base far from the endsUsually nothing, but now and then it creates a new false splice site, and part of the intron is kept in the message.

    Counting amino acids. Many proteins lose their first methionine soon after they are made. Textbooks, Lehninger among them, number such a protein from the amino acid after it, so amino acid n of the finished protein is coded by codon n + 1, counting the ATG as codon 1. Section 12 shows why this matters when you look a change up.

    Splice and translate

    A made-up gene: ATG GCC AAG | intron GTAAGTCTTTCAG | TGG GAA TAA

    Which loses more of a protein: a nonsense change near the start of the coding sequence, or one near the end?

    Near the start: the chain stops after only a few amino acids. (A stop codon early in the message also often marks the message itself for destruction, so almost no protein is made.) A stop near the end may leave a protein that still partly works.

    12Naming a change, and looking it up

    Laboratories describe changes in a standard language set by HGVS (the Human Genome Variation Society). It is what ClinVar uses, so you need to read it.

    WrittenMeans (all made up)
    c.58C>AIn the coding sequence (c.), base 58 is changed from C to A. Base c.1 is the A of the start codon ATG.
    c.135+1G>AIn the intron that follows coding base 135: its first base (+1), the G of its GT, is changed to A.
    c.136-2A>GTwo bases before coding base 136, at the end of the intron before it: the A of the intron's AG.
    c.50_53delBases 50 to 53 are deleted.
    p.Ala12AspIn the protein (p.), amino acid 12, alanine, is changed to aspartate. HGVS counts the first methionine as amino acid 1.
    p.Trp53TerAmino acid 53, tryptophan, becomes a stop (Ter, or *).

    Codon k (counting the ATG as codon 1) holds bases c.3k−2, c.3k−1 and c.3k. Older papers and many textbooks use other names: a change described by its codon, or by its place in an intron (called IVS, "intervening sequence", with the intron's number and the base's place in it). And for proteins that lose their first methionine, such as the globins, the traditional names do not count the methionine, so their numbers are one less than HGVS's p. numbers for the same change.

    Translate between the names

    In a made-up gene, codon 12 (counting the ATG as codon 1) changes from GCC to GAC. The protein loses its first methionine once it is made.

    ClinVar and HbVar

    ClinVar (at NCBI) collects what laboratories have reported about each human variant. Search it with the gene and the change, for example GENE c.58C>A with the gene's symbol in place of GENE, or a variant's traditional name. For each variant it gives:

    • its names, in HGVS for the DNA and the protein, and often its common name;
    • its clinical significance: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, of uncertain significance, likely benign or benign;
    • the condition it is linked to, and how it is inherited;
    • a review status, in stars: how many laboratories agree, and whether an expert panel has reviewed it.

    HbVar is a database of hemoglobin variants and thalassemia mutations alone, kept at Penn State. It lists each variant's traditional name, its effect on the protein and on the blood, and how common it is and where.

    A diagnosis puts the two alleles together: what each one does, and what the pair means for the person.

    A made-up protein loses its first methionine once it is made. A paper calls a change in it "Ser30Gly". What would its HGVS protein name be?

    p.Ser31Gly. The traditional name does not count the first methionine; HGVS does, so its number is one higher.

    13A protein in 3D

    The Protein Data Bank (PDB) holds the measured shapes of proteins: the position of every atom, mostly from X-ray crystallography. Each entry has a four-character ID. A viewer draws them in different styles: a cartoon, where α helices are ribbons and the chain is a smooth line, to show the fold; and sticks for chosen residues and small molecules, to show exactly where they sit.

    To practise, here is myoglobin (PDB 1MBO, from the sperm whale), the oxygen store of muscle and hemoglobin's single-chain relative. Each hemoglobin chain has the same fold, the globin fold: eight α helices, named A to H, wrapped round the heme. Two histidines matter: the proximal histidine (His93, on helix F) binds the iron from below, and the distal histidine (His64, on helix E) sits above it, beside the bound O2.

    In a viewer, a change at one residue of a chain is shown as sticks. What can the 3D structure tell you that the sequence alone cannot?

    Where that residue sits in the folded protein: buried inside or on the surface, near the heme or far from it, at a contact between chains, beside which other residues. A change on the surface can matter as much as one inside, if it makes a new contact with another molecule.
