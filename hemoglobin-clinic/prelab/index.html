The Hemoglobin Clinic: prelab review

This review explains the methods you will use: BLAST, PCR and its primers, setting up a reaction, a restriction digest, an agarose gel, Sanger sequencing, and reading a gene into a protein. Every sequence and number you practise on here is made up: none of it is the gene or the patient you will work on.

1. The plan of the lab

A patient's blood has come to the clinic's laboratory. Hemoglobin is made of two α chains and two β chains; the β chain is coded by one gene, and changes in it cause some of the commonest inherited diseases in the world. You will find out which form of that gene the patient carries on each of their two chromosomes.

  • Session 1. Identify the gene from a read of the patient's DNA (BLAST). Design primers. Set up a PCR of the patient's DNA beside a control and a tube with no DNA, and run it. Set up a digest of the products with a restriction enzyme, and send a tube of product for sequencing.
  • Session 2. Run the digests on an agarose gel and read the size of every band. Read the sequencing trace. Work out the protein, and the diagnosis.

2. BLAST: which gene is this?

BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, at NCBI) compares your sequence with every sequence in a database and lists the best matches. For each hit, read:

  • Description: the gene and the organism the hit comes from.
  • Per. Ident.: the share of aligned bases that are the same. 100% is a perfect match.
  • Query Cover: how much of your sequence the alignment covers.
  • E value: how many hits this good you would expect by chance in a database this size. The smaller, the more certain the match; for a long perfect match BLAST prints 0.0.
  • Accession: the database's name for the record (for example NM_ for an mRNA, NG_ for a gene's reference record).

A good identification has a high identity over nearly all of your sequence, a tiny E value, and a description that makes sense. A hit that matches only a short stretch, however well, is weaker evidence.

3. PCR and its primers

The polymerase chain reaction copies one stretch of DNA millions of times. Each cycle has three steps:

  1. Denature (about 95 °C): the two strands separate.
  2. Anneal (about 50 to 65 °C): the two primers bind to their places, one on each strand.
  3. Extend (72 °C): Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to each primer's 3′ end, copying the strand. Taq adds about 1000 bases a minute.

Each cycle roughly doubles the product, so 30 cycles turn a few thousand copies into far more than you need. Because both strands are copied from opposite ends, the product runs from the 5′ end of one primer to the 5′ end of the other.

Writing primers 5′ to 3′

Primers are written 5′ to 3′, like every DNA sequence. The forward primer is copied straight from the top strand, at the start of the stretch you want. The reverse primer binds the top strand at the far end, so it is the reverse complement of the top strand there: write the top-strand bases backwards, then swap A for T and G for C.

A made-up top strand reads, at the far end of a stretch you want to copy, 5′-GATTCCAGTTGCAAGCTGAC-3′.

Good primers

  • 18 to 25 bases, 40 to 60% G and C.
  • A melting temperature (Tm, where half the primer is bound) of about 57 to 63 °C, and the two primers within 2 to 3 °C of each other. Anneal a few degrees below the lower Tm: too hot and the primers do not stay bound; too cool and they also bind places that only nearly match.
  • A G or C at the 3′ end (a "clamp"), but not too many: the 3′ end is where the polymerase starts.
  • No runs of four or more of one base.
  • No pairing of the primers' 3′ ends with each other or themselves: such primers copy each other and make a short "primer-dimer".
  • Only one place in the genome. A related gene that is almost the same sequence is a trap: check every pair with Primer-BLAST, which searches the genome for other places the pair would copy.
  • Not on a base that differs between people, if you want to copy every version alike: a mismatch at a primer's 3′ end stops it.

4. Setting up a reaction

Every volume comes from C1V1 = C2V2: the stock's concentration times the volume you take equals the final concentration times the final volume. Water makes up the rest.

A made-up reaction of 50 µL needs a 5x buffer at 1x, a primer at 0.2 µM from a 10 µM stock, and 10 ng of DNA from a stock of 5 ng/µL.

Pipettes, tips and contamination

Choose the smallest pipette whose range holds the volume. A used tip carries a film of what it touched: put it into a stock and the stock now holds a trace of it, and every tube made from that stock afterwards. PCR finds even a few copies of DNA, so a fresh tip for every liquid is the rule. This is also why every PCR has a no-template control (all the reagents, no DNA): a band in it means something was contaminated. A positive control, DNA whose answer is already known, shows that the whole method works.

5. Restriction enzymes

A restriction enzyme cuts DNA wherever it finds its recognition sequence. A single changed base can create a site or destroy one, so cutting a PCR product and looking at the pieces on a gel can show which version of a gene a person carries.

HinfI cuts G^ANTC (N is any base; the cut is after the G). A made-up PCR product of 240 bp has HinfI sites whose cuts fall after base 70 and after base 190.

Restriction enzymes need their own buffer, their temperature (often 37 °C) and enough time; too little of any leaves some sites uncut, and a partial digest shows extra bands, the uncut pieces joined.

6. The agarose gel

DNA is negatively charged, so in an electric field it runs toward the positive (red) electrode: the wells go at the negative (black) end. The gel is a mesh: short pieces slip through faster than long ones. A weaker gel (1%) separates long DNA; a stronger one (2 to 3%) separates short DNA. The stain in the gel binds DNA and glows under blue light; an amber filter blocks the blue so the glow shows. Each sample goes in with loading dye, which is dense (it makes the sample sink into the well) and coloured (you can watch it run).

Reading sizes against the ladder

A ladder is a mixture of pieces of known sizes. Over the gel's useful range, the distance a piece runs is close to a straight line against the logarithm of its size. Measure every ladder band, fit log10(size) against distance, and read each unknown band's size from the line.

On a made-up gel, the ladder's 1000 bp band ran 20 mm and its 100 bp band 50 mm (a straight line of log10(size) against distance through these two).

7. Sanger sequencing

The core extends one primer along the template with a little of each of four fluorescent dideoxy-nucleotides: each stops a strand at an A, C, G or T, with its own colour. The stopped strands are sorted by length in a capillary, and a detector reads the colours as they pass: a trace of peaks, one base after another, read from the primer onward.

  • Before sequencing, a PCR product is cleaned up: an enzyme eats the leftover single-stranded primers, another spends the leftover dNTPs. The enzymes must then be killed by heat before the sequencing primer goes in, or they eat that too. Leftover PCR primers would read from both ends at once and make the trace a mixture.
  • The first 20 or so bases are poorly read; the trace fades after several hundred.
  • Heterozygote: where the two copies of a gene differ by one base, the trace shows two peaks of about half height at that position.
  • Homozygote: where both copies carry the same change, the trace shows one clean peak of the changed base. Only comparing with the reference sequence finds it.
  • A deletion in one copy: from the deletion on, the two copies are out of step and the trace becomes a mixture of two sequences.

8. From gene to protein

Most human genes are split: exons, which reach the mature mRNA, and introns between them, which are spliced out. Almost every intron begins with GT and ends with AG. Read the spliced message from the ATG in threes (codons) with the genetic code; a stop codon (TAA, TAG, TGA) ends the protein. A change can swap one amino acid for another, make a stop codon early, shift the reading frame (a deletion not a multiple of three), or break a splice site.

Many proteins lose their first methionine after they are made. Textbooks (Lehninger among them) number such a protein from the amino acid after it, so amino acid n is coded by codon n + 1 counted from the ATG.
